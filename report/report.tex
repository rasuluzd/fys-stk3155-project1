\title{Regression, resampling and gradient descent for the Runge function}
\author{Rasul Ruslanovitsj {\O}zdber}
\affiliation{Department of Physics, University of Oslo, Norway}
\date{October 5, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Polynomial regression provides a simple setting for studying how model complexity affects prediction. We investigated when regularization improves a noisy Runge fit and how reliably resampling and gradient methods identify suitable models. We implemented ordinary least squares, Ridge, bootstrap and gradient optimizers, using scikit-learn for cross-validation and reference fits and Autograd for derivative checks. With 100 observations and noise standard deviation 0.1, the lowest ten-fold cross-validation MSE was 0.0173, with a fold standard error of 0.0014. The selected OLS model gave an independent-test MSE of 0.0113; nearly unregularized Ridge gave essentially the same result. Stronger regularization mainly helped unstable high-degree fits. Bootstrap estimates were particularly sensitive to boundary coverage in small samples. Momentum reached the specified coefficient tolerance faster than the other tested optimizers on the degree-six problem. The results support selecting complexity by held-out error and comparing optimization methods at a stated accuracy and computational budget.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
\emph{The abstract and this section contain LLM-generated text; see Appendix~\ref{app:llm}.}

Polynomial models are easy to fit, but increasing their degree can amplify noise instead of improving prediction. Runge's function has a sharp central peak and a known target, making it useful for separating fitting error from noise. We studied this problem using OLS, Ridge and Lasso, following Project~1 and the regression and resampling chapters of the course notes \cite{Project1_2026,HjorthJensen2026}.

The project code implements OLS/Ridge, bootstrap and the gradient update rules. Scikit-learn supplies reference estimators and cross-validation; Autograd supplies automatic derivatives. We compared the effect of degree, noise, sample size, penalty and optimizer. Sections~\ref{sec:theory} and \ref{sec:implementation} describe the methods and checks. Section~\ref{sec:results} answers parts a--i, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses the findings. The code, settings and saved results are at \url{https://github.com/rasuluzd/fys-stk3155-project1}.

\section{Theory and methods}\label{sec:theory}
\emph{LLM-generated text; see Appendix~\ref{app:llm}.}

\subsection{Data, scaling and error measures}\label{sec:data}\label{sec:scaling}
Following part a, we generated
\begin{equation}
y_i=f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i,\quad f(x)=\frac{1}{1+25x^2},\quad
\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).
\end{equation}
Unless specified otherwise, 100 inputs were drawn uniformly from $[-1,1]$, with $\sigma=0.1$, seed 2026 and an 80/20 training/test split. We fitted polynomials through degree 15 and extended the sensitivity and model-selection studies to degree 20.

For degree $p$, the raw features are $A_{ij}=x_i^j$, $j=1,\ldots,p$. Using only the training observations, we computed column means $\mu_j$, standard deviations $s_j$ and target mean $\bar y$. The fitted prediction is
\begin{equation}
\widehat y(x)=\bar y+\sum_{j=1}^p\theta_j\frac{x^j-\mu_j}{s_j}.
\end{equation}
Centering handles the intercept separately, while standardization makes the slope penalties comparable across columns \cite[Sec.~3.13]{HjorthJensen2026}. The raw intercept is $\bar y-\sum_j\theta_j\mu_j/s_j$ and is unpenalized. Each test set uses its training means and scales. Scaling is refitted inside every bootstrap sample and CV fold to prevent held-out data influencing the fit. It reduces scale differences but cannot remove correlations between powers of $x$.

On each evaluation set we used
\begin{equation}
\MSE=\frac1n\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2,\qquad
R^2=1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}.
\end{equation}
Here $\bar y$ is the mean of that evaluation set. Negative $R^2$ means predictions are worse than its constant mean \cite[Sec.~3.5]{HjorthJensen2026}.

\subsection{OLS, Ridge and Lasso}
Write $X$ for the standardized training features, $y$ for the centered target and $n$ for the training size. The three costs are
\begin{align}
C_{\rm OLS}&=\frac1n\|y-X\theta\|^2,\nonumber\\
C_{\rm Ridge}&=C_{\rm OLS}+\lambda\|\theta\|_2^2,\nonumber\\
C_{\rm Lasso}&=C_{\rm OLS}+\lambda\|\theta\|_1.
\end{align}
Setting the OLS/Ridge gradients to zero gives
\begin{align}
\widehat\theta_{\rm OLS}&=X^+y,\nonumber\\
\widehat\theta_{\rm Ridge}&=(X^TX+n\lambda I)^{-1}X^Ty.
\end{align}
We evaluated these using the pseudoinverse/SVD, without explicitly inverting $X^TX$. For $X=UDV^T$, Ridge multiplies each OLS singular-direction coefficient by
\begin{equation}
q_j=\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+n\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Small singular values are damped most strongly, reducing variance at the cost of bias \cite[Secs.~3.3 and 3.8]{HjorthJensen2026}. Lasso can set slopes to zero, but has no corresponding general closed form \cite[Sec.~3.9]{HjorthJensen2026}. Our mean-loss convention requires scikit-learn parameters $\alpha=n\lambda$ for Ridge and $\alpha=\lambda/2$ for Lasso.

\subsection{Bias--variance decomposition and resampling}\label{sec:biasvar_theory}\label{sec:resampling}
Let $g_D(x)$ be a fit from random training data $D$, and $m(x)=\E_D[g_D(x)]$. At a fixed input, an independent test response is $Y=f(x)+\varepsilon$. Adding and subtracting $m$ gives
\[
Y-g_D=(f-m)+(m-g_D)+\varepsilon.
\]
After squaring and taking expectations, the cross terms vanish because $\E_D[m-g_D]=0$, the test noise has mean zero, and it is independent of $D$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
\E_{D,\varepsilon}[(Y-g_D)^2]
=\underbrace{(f-m)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
+\underbrace{\E_D[(g_D-m)^2]}_{\text{variance}}+\sigma^2.
\label{eq:biasvar}
\end{equation}
Averaging over evaluation inputs gives prediction MSE. Bias measures the mean fit's systematic error; variance measures its sensitivity to training data; $\sigma^2$ is new observation noise. The training-data expectation includes both random input locations and training noise \cite[Sec.~2.10]{HjorthJensen2026}.

The bootstrap estimates training-set variation by resampling paired training observations with replacement. Each resample is fitted and predicted on the same fixed test inputs \cite[Sec.~2.11]{HjorthJensen2026}. With $B$ predictions $g_{bi}$ and their mean $\bar g_i$, the stored arrays obey
\begin{align}
\frac1{n_t B}\sum_{i,b}(y_i-g_{bi})^2
={}&\frac1{n_t}\sum_i(y_i-\bar g_i)^2\nonumber\\
&+\frac1{n_t B}\sum_{i,b}(g_{bi}-\bar g_i)^2.
\end{align}
The first term uses noisy $y$, so it includes the noise contribution in expectation. Its difference from the same quantity using known $f$ is
\[
\frac1{n_t}\sum_i\left[\varepsilon_i^2+2\varepsilon_i(f_i-\bar g_i)\right].
\]
This averages to $\sigma^2$ over independent test noise, but need not equal it for one fixed test set. We show both versions.

In $k$-fold CV, each fold is predicted by a model fitted to the other $k-1$ folds. We used shuffled five- and ten-fold splits, identical across candidates, and selected the lowest mean validation MSE \cite[Secs.~2.12 and 3.14]{HjorthJensen2026}. Fold standard errors are descriptive because training folds overlap.

\subsection{Gradient methods}\label{sec:gd_theory}
The analytical gradients and Ridge Hessian are
\begin{align}
\nabla C_{\rm OLS}&=\frac2nX^T(X\theta-y),\nonumber\\
\nabla C_{\rm Ridge}&=\nabla C_{\rm OLS}+2\lambda\theta,\nonumber\\
H&=\frac2nX^TX+2\lambda I.
\end{align}
Plain GD updates $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\nabla C(\theta_k)$. Each quadratic error component is multiplied by $1-\eta h_j$, so convergence requires $0<\eta<2/h_{\max}$ on a positive-definite problem. The best worst-direction rate is $2/(h_{\max}+h_{\min})$ \cite[Secs.~4.3--4.5]{HjorthJensen2026}.

Momentum keeps a decaying sum of previous gradients. AdaGrad divides by accumulated squared gradients; RMSprop uses a moving average instead. Adam combines moving averages of gradients and their squares, with bias correction. We used the course updates, with momentum 0.9, RMSprop decay 0.99, Adam decays 0.9 and 0.999, and denominator offset $10^{-8}$ \cite[Secs.~4.6 and 4.8--4.11]{HjorthJensen2026,HjorthJensen2026exercises}.

Autograd differentiates the implemented scalar cost by applying the chain rule through its operations. It uses neither symbolic simplification nor finite-difference perturbations. Reverse mode obtains all parameter derivatives in one backward pass, although overhead can dominate small problems \cite[Sec.~4.14]{HjorthJensen2026,Maclaurin2015}.

For Lasso we add $\lambda\operatorname{sign}(\theta)$ to the OLS gradient and choose $\operatorname{sign}(0)=0$. This is a valid subgradient because the subdifferential of $|\theta|$ at zero is $[-1,1]$. It does not guarantee exact zeros at a finite iteration.

SGD reshuffles the training observations each epoch and applies the same update rules to mini-batch gradients, dividing the data term by the actual batch size. Optimizer state persists between batches. We compared constant rates with $\eta_t=\eta_0t_1/(t+t_1)$, where $t$ counts updates from zero \cite[Sec.~4.7]{HjorthJensen2026,HjorthJensen2026week39}.

\section{Implementation and verification}\label{sec:implementation}
\emph{LLM-generated text; see Appendix~\ref{app:llm}.}

The repository separates \texttt{regression.py}, \texttt{resampling.py} and \texttt{optimizers.py}; task scripts write JSON results and PDF figures. The numerical work uses NumPy, scikit-learn and Matplotlib \cite{Harris2020,Pedregosa2011,Hunter2007}. The README lists dependencies, all scripts and reproduction commands. Numerical claims below identify their saved results in source comments.

The 25 tests compare custom routines with closed-form or library answers. Benchmark prediction differences were $7.6\times10^{-14}$ for OLS and zero to reported precision for Ridge. Relative analytical/AD gradient disagreement was $1.6\times10^{-15}$. Full-batch SGD agreed with GD within $2.2\times10^{-16}$; custom CV matched scikit-learn when folds and preprocessing matched. The OLS library reference uses the same relative singular-value cutoff, $10^{-15}$, as the custom pseudoinverse.

All iterative comparisons start at zero. Unless stated otherwise, convergence means relative coefficient error $r=\|\theta-\widehat\theta\|/\|\widehat\theta\|<10^{-6}$. This measures agreement with a known solution; it is distinct from test error. Verification scope and environment are recorded in \texttt{results/verification\_audit.json}.

\subsection{a) Ordinary least squares}\label{sec:res_ols}
% Evidence: part_a.json/main, scaling, ensembles; part_a_ols.py.
On the main split, test MSE was 0.01894 at degree 12 and 0.01899 at degree 6 (Fig.~\ref{fig:mse_r2}). This difference is too small to establish a unique optimum from 20 test points. Training error continued to fall, while high-degree test error increased. The corresponding fall in test $R^2$ shows that fitting training noise does not improve prediction.

The standardized coefficient norm rose from 2.70 at degree 6 to 856 at degree 15 (Fig.~\ref{fig:coef}). Large coefficients cancel to form the fitted curve, making it sensitive to data perturbations. At degree 15, standardization reduced the design condition number from $4.7\times10^5$ to $1.9\times10^5$, but the columns remained strongly correlated. This explains why scaling alone did not cure the instability.

Over 200 datasets per setting, the degree giving the lowest median test MSE increased from 10 to 18 when total sample size increased from 50 to 1000 at $\sigma=0.1$. At $N=100$, increasing noise from 0.05 to 0.3 moved it from degree 16 to 8. More data supported greater complexity; more noise favored simpler fits. These are median results: rare unstable fits can make the mean much larger.

\subsection{b) Ridge regression}\label{sec:res_ridge}
% Evidence: part_b.json/main, svd_degree15; ridge_supplement.json/main, ensemble.
Ridge used degrees 1--20 and 45 penalties from $10^{-10}$ to 10. The main-split minimum test MSE was 0.01347 at degree 10 and $\lambda=1.78\times10^{-6}$, below OLS's 0.01894. This is an exploratory grid minimum; part i uses CV and a separate test set for final selection.

Figures~\ref{fig:ridge_metrics} and \ref{fig:ridge_coefficients} repeat the scores and coefficient analysis at fixed penalties. Increasing $\lambda$ limits the large coefficients, but excessive shrinkage increases both training and test error. At degree 15, singular values ranged from 27.59 to $1.49\times10^{-4}$. The factors $q_j$ suppress these small, unstable directions most strongly, explaining Ridge's wider usable degree range.

In 40 paired datasets per setting, degree-20 Ridge with $\lambda=10^{-7}$ reduced median test MSE from 0.236 to 0.0183 at $N=50$, $\sigma=0.1$. At $N=1000$, the values were 0.01049 for OLS and 0.01083 for Ridge. At $N=100$ without noise, Ridge gave 0.00101 against OLS's $6.17\times10^{-5}$. Shrinkage helps when variance dominates, but can add unnecessary bias when the data already constrain the fit.

\subsection{c) Bias, variance and bootstrap}\label{sec:res_biasvar}
% Evidence: part_c.json/train_test,n=100,n=1000; part_c_bias_variance.py.
Figure~\ref{fig:hastie} compares 100 independent training sets of 80 observations, scored on 2000 fixed noisy test observations. Mean test MSE was lowest at degree 8, at 0.01656; median MSE favored degree 12, at 0.01339. The difference reflects occasional very inaccurate high-degree fits. The average training error fell throughout.

The bootstrap used 200 resamples per degree, with a reference of 400 independently generated training sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:biasvar}). With 80 training points, bootstrap error selected degree 4 and the fresh-set reference selected degree 8. At degree 8, variance estimates were 7.754 and 0.00597. Resampling can omit boundary observations, leaving a high-degree polynomial poorly constrained there. This particular bootstrap sample therefore gave a pessimistic estimate of instability.

With 800 training points, the degree-12 variance estimates were much closer: $1.79\times10^{-4}$ and $1.81\times10^{-4}$. More observations reduced the variance, allowing higher degree. Using noisy $y$ instead of known $f$ raised the measured squared bias by 0.01030, close to $\sigma^2=0.01$. The finite-sample noise cross term explains the remaining difference. The curves illustrate the bias--variance trade-off, but small-sample resampling need not locate it accurately.

\subsection{e) Gradient descent and automatic derivatives}\label{sec:res_gd}
% Evidence: part_e.json/gradient_check,timing,degree6; verification_supplement.json/ridge_gradient_sources.
For OLS and Ridge at degrees 6 and 15, analytical and AD gradients differed by at most $1.18\times10^{-15}$ relatively over 100 random coefficient vectors. Both derivative sources gave the same iteration counts: 14\,399 for degree-6 OLS and 9\,309 for Ridge at $\lambda=10^{-3}$, using the Hessian-based rates. Their final vectors differed by at most $4.44\times10^{-15}$. AD cost 16.1 objective evaluations at $n=80$, falling to 2.81 at $n=10^6$; Python/library overhead matters for small arrays.

For degree-6 OLS, $h_{\max}=7.69648$ and $h_{\min}=0.00368262$, giving $\eta<0.259859$. The rate $\eta_*=0.259735$ reached the target in 14\,399 iterations, exactly as predicted by the quadratic error recurrence. Smaller rates progressed slowly; rates above the bound amplified the steepest direction. Ridge with $\lambda=0.1$ reduced the condition number from 2090 to 38.8 and required 244 iterations. Regularization improved conditioning as well as changing the statistical fit.

\subsection{g) Lasso with gradient updates}\label{sec:res_lasso}
% Evidence: part_g.json/autograd_abs_derivative,methods_degree10; lasso_course_comparison.json.
Autograd returned zero for the derivative of $|\theta|$ at zero, which is a valid subgradient. We used this convention with all five update rules on the degree-10 problem at $\lambda=10^{-3}$. Each run used 40\,000 updates and eight fixed candidate rates: seven logarithmic values from $10^{-4}$ to 0.1, plus $1/L$. Rates minimized mean training objective gap over the last 200 updates; test error did not select them.

Table~\ref{tab:lasso} compares final iterates with coordinate-descent Lasso at $\alpha=\lambda/2$. Adam was closest, followed by momentum. None produced exact zeros, while the reference had three: a small coefficient and an exact zero are different numerical outcomes. The comparison with OLS and Ridge also depends on the accuracy criterion. After 10\,000 degree-10 GD steps, OLS had test MSE 0.01990 despite coefficient error 0.990; exact OLS gave 0.03702. A good prediction does not establish optimizer convergence.

\subsection{h) Stochastic gradient descent}\label{sec:res_sgd}

For decay, $t_1$ corresponds to 250 epochs. Constant-rate SGD fluctuated near the solution because individual batch gradients need not vanish there. \subsection{i) Final model selection}\label{sec:res_final}
% Evidence: part_i.json/grids,sigma=0.1,sigma=0.3,bias_variance_degree15,lasso_convergence_cv; model_selection_check.json.
Ten-fold CV used degrees 1--20, 21 Ridge penalties from $10^{-10}$ to 1, and seven Lasso penalties from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$. Each selected model was refitted on all 100 observations and evaluated on 2000 independent observations generated with seed 2027. The test set was used only after selection.

Table~\ref{tab:final} favors degree-12 OLS at $\sigma=0.1$. Ridge's CV score differs by only $1.8\times10^{-6}$, far below the descriptive fold standard error; its selected penalty is practically zero. OLS is therefore a reasonable choice without an additional tuning parameter. At $\sigma=0.3$, Ridge has the smallest CV MSE, but the three models' differences are again small compared with fold uncertainty. Increasing noise favors lower selected degrees.

Regularization mainly reduced variance here. In 150 independent degree-15 fits with 80 observations, OLS squared bias and variance were 0.00654 and 0.21098. At $\lambda=3.16\times10^{-4}$, Ridge gave 0.00670 and 0.00349, while Lasso gave 0.00487 and 0.00363. These known-function calculations explain the effect of the penalties; they did not select the models in the table. Degree selection controls OLS complexity; Ridge damps unstable directions; Lasso also removes coefficients. None changes the observation noise.

The Lasso search has a numerical limitation: 338 of 3080 fold/refit models reached their iteration limit. Stricter checks of the two minimum-CV Lasso selections changed CV scores by at most 0.045\%, but did not rerank every candidate. The small score differences and this limitation prevent a strong general ranking of the estimators.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
\emph{LLM-generated text; see Appendix~\ref{app:llm}.}

Increasing degree initially improved the Runge fit, but high-degree OLS became sensitive to noise and boundary coverage. Ridge reduced this instability by damping small singular directions. At the lower noise level, CV selected an OLS fit whose independent-test MSE was 0.0113, and regularization gave little further improvement. The penalties were most useful for protecting high-degree fits, rather than guaranteeing a better selected model.

The small-sample bootstrap was sensitive to lost boundary points, and the preferred degree varied between fold assignments. Optimizer rankings also depended on the rate grid, tolerance and budget: momentum was fastest in the degree-six deterministic comparison, while SGD's cheaper updates incurred more overhead. The finite samples and incompletely converged Lasso candidates limit the strength of the model ranking.

A next step is repeated outer validation, which would measure how stable the selected model is across new training samples. Fully converging the Lasso candidate grid would separate solver error from statistical differences. These two improvements would address the main uncertainties without adding more model types.
