\begin{abstract}
Polynomial regression on a known function is a convenient test bed for choosing model complexity and for checking how far resampling estimates of the error can be trusted. We fitted Runge's function, whose polynomial interpolants oscillate strongly near the ends of the interval, to 100 noisy observations. Ordinary least squares (OLS), Ridge regression, the bootstrap and plain, momentum, adaptive and stochastic gradient descent were implemented from scratch, while scikit-learn was used for cross-validation and reference fits and Autograd for automatic derivatives. Ten-fold cross-validation selected a degree-12 OLS fit with MSE $0.0173\pm0.0014$. On 2000 independent observations its MSE was 0.0113, close to the noise variance of 0.01, and Ridge with an almost vanishing penalty did equally well. The high-degree fits failed mainly where they had to extrapolate beyond the outermost training points. These few points dominated the single-split test errors and the small-sample bootstrap variance, and Ridge removed most of this instability. With full-batch gradients, momentum reached a fixed coefficient tolerance fastest, while Adam was the least sensitive to the learning rate and the most accurate with mini-batches and for Lasso. Our results suggest choosing the complexity by cross-validated error, and comparing optimizers at a stated accuracy and cost rather than by their test error.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Fitting a function to noisy data is the basic task of supervised learning, and polynomial regression is the simplest version of it. The model is linear in its parameters, so ordinary least squares (OLS) and Ridge regression have closed-form solutions that any iterative method can be checked against \cite{HjorthJensen2026,Hastie2009}. As test function we use Runge's function $f(x)=1/(1+25x^2)$ on $[-1,1]$. Runge showed that interpolating this function with polynomials at equally spaced points diverges near the ends of the interval as the degree grows \cite{Runge1901}. With noisy, randomly placed data the same problem shows up as variance. A high-degree polynomial is held in place by only a few observations near $\pm1$, and where it has to extrapolate it can swing far away from the data. Since $f$ is known, we can measure the bias and the variance of a fit directly instead of only estimating them, which makes the problem well suited for studying the bias--variance trade-off.

The project covers three groups of methods. Ridge \cite{Hoerl1970} and Lasso \cite{Tibshirani1996} regression add a penalty on the coefficients and accept some bias in return for lower variance. The bootstrap \cite{Efron1979} and $k$-fold cross-validation \cite{Hastie2009} estimate the test error without new data, and we use them to choose the degree and the penalty. Finally, gradient descent replaces the closed-form solutions. We test plain gradient descent, momentum \cite{Polyak1964}, the adaptive methods AdaGrad \cite{Duchi2011}, RMSprop \cite{Tieleman2012} and Adam \cite{Kingma2015}, and stochastic mini-batches \cite{Robbins1951}. Gradient descent is also how the neural networks in Project~2 will be trained, and there no closed form exists.

Following the description of Project~1 in FYS-STK4155 \cite{Project1_2026}, we look at four questions: how the degree, sample size and noise level set the trade-off for OLS; when Ridge and Lasso do better than OLS; whether the bootstrap and cross-validation pick the same model complexity; and how efficiently the gradient methods reproduce the closed-form solutions. We wrote our own code for OLS and Ridge (via the singular value decomposition), the bootstrap, the gradient-based optimizers and a subgradient Lasso, with LLM assistance as declared in Appendix~\ref{app:llm}. Scikit-learn \cite{Pedregosa2011} was used for cross-validation and reference fits, and Autograd \cite{Maclaurin2015} for automatic derivatives.

Section~\ref{sec:theory} presents the models, the bias--variance decomposition and the optimizers, and Sec.~\ref{sec:implementation} describes the implementation and how it was tested against closed-form and library results. Section~\ref{sec:results} goes through parts a--i of the project, and Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusion} sums up the findings, their limitations and possible extensions. All code, settings and saved results are available at \url{https://github.com/rasuluzd/fys-stk3155-project1}.

\section{Theory and methods}\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Data, scaling and error measures}\label{sec:data}\label{sec:scaling}
Following part a, we generated
\begin{equation}
y_i=f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i,\quad f(x)=\frac{1}{1+25x^2},\quad
\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).
\end{equation}
Unless stated otherwise, 100 inputs were drawn uniformly from $[-1,1]$, with $\sigma=0.1$, seed 2026 and an 80/20 split into training and test data. We fitted polynomials up to degree 15, and up to degree 20 in the sensitivity and model-selection studies.

For degree $p$, the raw features are $A_{ij}=x_i^j$, $j=1,\ldots,p$. From the training observations only, we computed the column means $\mu_j$, the standard deviations $s_j$ and the target mean $\bar y$. The fitted prediction is
\begin{equation}
\widehat y(x)=\bar y+\sum_{j=1}^p\theta_j\frac{x^j-\mu_j}{s_j}.
\end{equation}
% Evidence: part_a.json/scaling (column standard deviations); benchmarks.json (scaled vs centred OLS).
Centering removes the intercept from the fit, so it is never penalized; the raw intercept is $\bar y-\sum_j\theta_j\mu_j/s_j$ \cite[Sec.~3.13]{HjorthJensen2026}. On $[-1,1]$ standardization matters less than for most data sets, since every monomial is bounded by one. At degree 15 the column standard deviations lie between 0.11 and 0.50. OLS predictions do not change at all when the columns are rescaled, and with and without standardization they agreed to $1.3\times10^{-13}$. We still scale, for two reasons. The Ridge and Lasso penalties act on the size of the coefficients, which are only comparable after standardization, and the largest stable step size in gradient descent depends on the spectrum of $X^TX$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:gd_theory}). The means and standard deviations are computed from the training data and then applied to the test data. They are recomputed inside every bootstrap sample and CV fold, since statistics that include held-out points would leak information about those points into the fit. Scaling does not remove the strong correlation between neighbouring powers of $x$, so the design matrix stays ill-conditioned at high degree.

On each evaluation set we used
\begin{equation}
\MSE=\frac1n\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2,\qquad
R^2=1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}.
\end{equation}
Here $\bar y$ is the mean of that evaluation set, so a negative $R^2$ means that the predictions are worse than simply using this mean \cite[Sec.~3.5]{HjorthJensen2026}.

\subsection{OLS, Ridge and Lasso}
Let $X$ be the standardized training features, $y$ the centered target and $n$ the number of training points. The three cost functions are
\begin{align}
C_{\rm OLS}&=\frac1n\|y-X\theta\|^2,\nonumber\\
C_{\rm Ridge}&=C_{\rm OLS}+\lambda\|\theta\|_2^2,\nonumber\\
C_{\rm Lasso}&=C_{\rm OLS}+\lambda\|\theta\|_1.
\end{align}
The OLS gradient $-\frac2nX^T(y-X\theta)$ vanishes when $X^TX\theta=X^Ty$. Ridge adds $2\lambda\theta$ to the gradient and hence $n\lambda I$ to $X^TX$ \cite{Hoerl1970}. The solutions are
\begin{align}
\widehat\theta_{\rm OLS}&=X^+y,\nonumber\\
\widehat\theta_{\rm Ridge}&=(X^TX+n\lambda I)^{-1}X^Ty.
\end{align}
We computed both through the pseudoinverse/SVD and never inverted $X^TX$ explicitly. For $X=UDV^T$, Ridge multiplies each OLS coefficient along a singular direction by
\begin{equation}
q_j=\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+n\lambda}.
\end{equation}
The directions with small singular values are damped the most, which lowers the variance at the cost of some bias \cite[Secs.~3.3 and 3.8]{HjorthJensen2026}. Lasso can set slopes exactly to zero, but in general it has no closed-form solution \cite{Tibshirani1996}, \cite[Sec.~3.9]{HjorthJensen2026}. Because our costs use the mean rather than the sum of squared errors, the matching scikit-learn parameters are $\alpha=n\lambda$ for Ridge and $\alpha=\lambda/2$ for Lasso.

A Lasso solution satisfies the stationarity conditions $g_j+\lambda\operatorname{sign}(\theta_j)=0$ for nonzero slopes and $|g_j|\leq\lambda$ for zero slopes, where $g$ is the gradient of the data term \cite[Sec.~3.9]{HjorthJensen2026}. We only used scikit-learn's coordinate-descent fits as references when these conditions and the duality gap were satisfied to $10^{-7}$ \cite{SklearnLasso}.

\subsection{Bias--variance decomposition and resampling}\label{sec:biasvar_theory}\label{sec:resampling}
Let $g_D(x)$ be the fit obtained from a random training set $D$, so that $\tilde y=g_D(x)$ in the notation of the project, and let $m(x)=\E_D[g_D(x)]$ be the mean fit. At a fixed input $x$ a new observation is $Y=f(x)+\varepsilon$, with $\E[\varepsilon]=0$, $\operatorname{var}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2$ and $\varepsilon$ independent of $D$. Adding and subtracting $m$ gives
\[
Y-g_D=(f-m)+(m-g_D)+\varepsilon,
\]
and squaring gives
\begin{align}
(Y-g_D)^2={}&(f-m)^2+(m-g_D)^2+\varepsilon^2\nonumber\\
&+2(f-m)(m-g_D)+2(f-m)\varepsilon\nonumber\\
&+2(m-g_D)\varepsilon .
\end{align}
We take the expectation over both $D$ and $\varepsilon$. Since $f-m$ does not depend on $D$, the first cross term averages to $2(f-m)\,\E_D[m-g_D]=0$ by the definition of $m$. The second vanishes because $\E[\varepsilon]=0$, and the third because $\varepsilon$ is independent of $D$, so that $\E[(m-g_D)\varepsilon]=\E_D[m-g_D]\,\E[\varepsilon]=0$. Hence
\begin{equation}
\E_{D,\varepsilon}[(Y-g_D)^2]
=\underbrace{(f-m)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
+\underbrace{\E_D[(g_D-m)^2]}_{\text{variance}}+\sigma^2.
\label{eq:biasvar}
\end{equation}
Averaging each term over the evaluation inputs gives the decomposition $\E[(y-\tilde y)^2]=\mathrm{Bias}^2[\tilde y]+\operatorname{var}[\tilde y]+\sigma^2$ used in the project, Eq.~(2.52) of the lecture notes \cite[Sec.~2.10]{HjorthJensen2026}. The squared bias is the systematic error of the mean fit, and the variance measures how much the fit changes from one training set to another. The last term, $\sigma^2$, is the noise in the new observation, which no model can remove. Note that $\E_D$ averages over training sets, that is, over both the random input locations and the training noise.

The bootstrap estimates how much the fit varies between training sets by resampling the training pairs with replacement \cite{Efron1979}. On average a resample contains only $1-(1-1/n)^n\approx63\%$ of the distinct observations, so each observation is missing from about 37\% of the resamples. Each resample is fitted and then predicted on the same fixed test inputs \cite[Secs.~2.10 and 2.11]{HjorthJensen2026}, as in the week~36 exercises \cite{Exercises36}. With $B$ predictions $g_{bi}$ and their mean $\bar g_i$, the stored arrays satisfy
\begin{align}
\frac1{n_t B}\sum_{i,b}(y_i-g_{bi})^2
={}&\frac1{n_t}\sum_i(y_i-\bar g_i)^2\nonumber\\
&+\frac1{n_t B}\sum_{i,b}(g_{bi}-\bar g_i)^2.
\end{align}
The first term on the right is computed with the noisy $y$, so on average it also contains the noise. It differs from the same quantity computed with the known $f$ by
\[
\frac1{n_t}\sum_i\left[\varepsilon_i^2+2\varepsilon_i(f_i-\bar g_i)\right],
\]
which averages to $\sigma^2$ over independent test noise but need not equal $\sigma^2$ for one fixed test set. We therefore show both versions.

In $k$-fold CV, each fold is predicted by a model fitted to the other $k-1$ folds \cite[Sec.~7.10]{Hastie2009}. We used shuffled five- and ten-fold splits, the same for all candidate models, and selected the model with the lowest mean validation MSE \cite[Secs.~2.12 and 3.14]{HjorthJensen2026}. Since the training folds overlap, the fold standard errors are only descriptive.

\subsection{Gradient methods}\label{sec:gd_theory}
The analytical gradients and the Ridge Hessian are
\begin{align}
\nabla C_{\rm OLS}&=\frac2nX^T(X\theta-y),\nonumber\\
\nabla C_{\rm Ridge}&=\nabla C_{\rm OLS}+2\lambda\theta,\nonumber\\
H&=\frac2nX^TX+2\lambda I.
\end{align}
Plain GD updates $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\nabla C(\theta_k)$. For a quadratic cost, the error along each eigenvector of $H$ is multiplied by $1-\eta h_j$ in every step, so on a positive-definite problem convergence requires $0<\eta<2/h_{\max}$. The best constant step size is $2/(h_{\max}+h_{\min})$ \cite[Secs.~4.3--4.5]{HjorthJensen2026}; the week~37 exercises check this bound numerically \cite{Exercises37}.

With $g_k=\nabla C(\theta_{k-1})$, each method subtracts a step $d_k$ from $\theta_{k-1}$:
\begin{align}
\text{Momentum:}\quad d_k&=\beta d_{k-1}+\eta g_k,\nonumber\\
\text{AdaGrad:}\quad a_k&=a_{k-1}+g_k^2,\nonumber\\
\text{RMSprop:}\quad a_k&=\rho a_{k-1}+(1-\rho)g_k^2.
\end{align}
For both adaptive methods the step is $d_k=\eta g_k/(\sqrt{a_k}+\epsilon)$. The AdaGrad denominator keeps growing, while RMSprop gradually forgets old squared gradients. Adam instead uses
\begin{align}
m_k&=\beta_1m_{k-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_k,\nonumber\\
v_k&=\beta_2v_{k-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_k^2,\nonumber\\
d_k&=\eta\frac{m_k/(1-\beta_1^k)}{\sqrt{v_k/(1-\beta_2^k)}+\epsilon}.
\end{align}
All operations are componentwise, the state is initialized to zero, and $k=1$ on the first update. Momentum \cite{Polyak1964} keeps a memory of the previous step, while AdaGrad \cite{Duchi2011} and RMSprop \cite{Tieleman2012} rescale each coordinate by its gradient history. Adam \cite{Kingma2015} combines the two ideas and corrects for its moving averages being too small in the first iterations. We used $\beta=0.9$, $\rho=0.99$, $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.999)$ and $\epsilon=10^{-8}$ \cite[Secs.~4.6 and 4.8--4.11]{HjorthJensen2026}. As in the code of the lecture notes \cite[Sec.~4.12]{HjorthJensen2026} and the week~38 lecture \cite{Week38Tuesday}, $\epsilon$ is added outside the square root. The comparisons follow the week~37 and 38 exercises \cite{Exercises37,Exercises38}.

Autograd differentiates the implemented cost function by applying the chain rule to the elementary operations it records while the cost is evaluated \cite{Maclaurin2015,Baydin2018}. This is different from symbolic differentiation, which manipulates expressions and can produce very large formulas, and from finite differences, which suffer from truncation and round-off errors. AD gives the exact derivative of the program, up to floating-point rounding. In reverse mode, one forward and one backward pass give all $p$ partial derivatives at the cost of a small constant times one cost evaluation, independent of $p$ \cite{Baydin2018}, \cite[Sec.~4.14]{HjorthJensen2026}. We chose Autograd over JAX because it works directly on our NumPy cost functions and uses 64-bit precision by default, so the analytical and automatic gradients could be compared at machine precision without any extra setup.

For Lasso we add $\lambda\operatorname{sign}(\theta)$ to the OLS gradient, with $\operatorname{sign}(0)=0$. This is a valid subgradient, since the subdifferential of $|\theta|$ at zero is the whole interval $[-1,1]$; the value 1 that JAX returns in the project notebook is an equally valid choice \cite{Project1_2026}. A fixed-step subgradient method will, however, not produce exact zeros after a finite number of iterations.

SGD \cite{Robbins1951} reshuffles the training data every epoch and applies the same update rules to mini-batch gradients, with the data term divided by the actual batch size. The optimizer state is kept from one batch to the next. We compared constant rates with the schedule $\eta_t=\eta_0t_1/(t+t_1)$, where $t$ counts updates from zero \cite[Sec.~4.7]{HjorthJensen2026}, as in the week~38 and 39 material \cite{Exercises38,HjorthJensen2026week39}.

\section{Implementation and verification}\label{sec:implementation}
The code is split into three modules, \texttt{regression.py}, \texttt{resampling.py} and \texttt{optimizers.py}, and one script per project part that writes its results to JSON and its figures to PDF. We used NumPy, scikit-learn and Matplotlib \cite{Harris2020,Pedregosa2011,Hunter2007}. The README lists the dependencies with versions, all scripts and the commands needed to reproduce the results, and the JSON file behind each number in this report is noted in a comment in the LaTeX source.

Every fit in the resampling experiments goes through the same four steps. The order matters, since it is what keeps the held-out data out of the fit:
\begin{enumerate}
\item choose the training rows: an 80/20 split, a bootstrap resample or $k-1$ CV folds;
\item build the columns $x,\dots,x^p$ and compute their means and standard deviations, and the mean of $y$, from these rows only;
\item fit $\theta$ on the scaled training rows, by the SVD, a gradient method or scikit-learn;
\item transform the held-out rows with the training statistics, predict and store the error.
\end{enumerate}
All gradient methods share one driver loop in \texttt{optimizers.py}, and only the update rule changes:
{\small
\begin{verbatim}
for k in range(n_iter):
    g = gradient(theta)        # analytic or AD
    theta = theta - opt.update(g, eta)
    if rel_error(theta, theta_ref) < tol:
        break
\end{verbatim}}
\noindent Here \texttt{opt.update} returns $\eta g$ for plain GD and the step $d_k$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:gd_theory} for the other methods. SGD runs the same update on each mini-batch.

% Evidence: benchmarks.json; part_d.json/own_vs_sklearn.
The test suite has 27 tests that compare our routines with closed-form or library results. OLS predictions agreed with scikit-learn to $7.6\times10^{-14}$, and the Ridge coefficients agreed to the reported precision. The analytical and AD gradients agreed to a relative $1.6\times10^{-15}$. Full-batch SGD reproduced GD to within $2.2\times10^{-16}$, and our $k$-fold code reproduced scikit-learn's fold scores to a relative $1.2\times10^{-9}$ when the folds and the preprocessing were the same. For the OLS comparison, the library reference uses the same relative singular-value cutoff as our pseudoinverse, $10^{-15}$.

All GD and SGD runs start from $\theta=0$, while the library Lasso penalty paths use warm starts. Unless stated otherwise, we say that a method has converged when the relative coefficient error $r=\|\theta-\widehat\theta\|/\|\widehat\theta\|$ is below $10^{-6}$. Note that $r$ measures the distance to a known solution and is not the same thing as the test error.

\subsection{a) Ordinary least squares}\label{sec:res_ols}

Figure~\ref{fig:mse_r2} shows the usual bias--variance picture \cite[Secs.~2.9 and 2.10]{HjorthJensen2026}. The training error falls with every added degree, as it must, since each model contains the previous one. The test error first drops and then becomes erratic at high degree. On the main split, degrees 6 and 12 give practically the same test MSE (0.0190 and 0.0189), and with only 20 test points we cannot say that one is better than the other.

Figure~\ref{fig:edge} shows where the high-degree fits go wrong. Three of the 20 test points lie to the left of the smallest training input, $x=-0.953$, so every model has to extrapolate there, and this is where a high-degree polynomial is least constrained. At degrees 13 and 15 these three points account for 89\% and 92\% of the test error. Without them, the test MSE stays between 0.014 and 0.018 for all degrees from 8 to 15. The spikes in Fig.~\ref{fig:mse_r2} are therefore Runge's phenomenon at the edge of the interval \cite{Runge1901}, and not a general loss of accuracy.

The coefficients in Fig.~\ref{fig:coef} point the same way. In panel (a) the even coefficients $\theta_2$, $\theta_4$ and $\theta_6$ alternate in sign and grow steadily with the degree, and the norm in panel (b) grows from 2.7 at degree 6 to 856 at degree 15. The fit turns into a balance between large terms of opposite sign, and small changes in the data shift that balance. Panel (b) also splits the norm into even and odd powers. Since $f$ is even, the odd powers are only needed because the random sample is not symmetric, and for even degrees up to 12 they carry less than 10\% of the norm. Their share is larger at odd degrees and reaches 82\% at degree 15, where most of the coefficient norm sits in odd powers. The same asymmetry keeps the odd Lasso coefficients in part g from vanishing. Standardization does little to prevent the growth. It only lowers the condition number of the degree-15 design matrix from $4.7\times10^5$ for the raw powers, or $3.0\times10^5$ after centering, to $1.9\times10^5$, because the powers $x^j$ stay strongly correlated on $[-1,1]$.

To see how the best degree depends on the data, we repeated the experiment on 200 data sets per setting. With $\sigma=0.1$, the degree with the lowest median test MSE went from 10 for 50 points to 18 for 1000 points, and the spread between data sets shrank: at the best degree, the interquartile range of the test MSE was 0.011--0.024 for 50 points and 0.0096--0.0110 for 1000 points. With 100 points, the best degree dropped from 16 to 8 when the noise was increased from 0.05 to 0.3. More data can support a more complex model, while more noise favours a simpler one. We use the median here because a few unstable fits make the mean very large.

\subsection{b) Ridge regression}\label{sec:res_ridge}
% Evidence: part_b.json/main, singular_values_degree15; ridge_supplement.json/main, ensemble.
\begin{figure*}[tb]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{b_ridge.pdf}
\caption{Ridge regression with fixed penalties on the seed-2026 split. (a) Test MSE, (b) training MSE and (c) norm of the standardized coefficients against degree for OLS and four values of $\lambda$. The penalty keeps the high-degree test error bounded and shrinks the coefficients by orders of magnitude, at the price of a higher training error for large $\lambda$.}\label{fig:ridge}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:ridge} repeats the analysis of part a for four fixed penalties. Ridge keeps the high-degree test error under control. At degree 20, OLS has a test MSE of about $9\times10^3$, while Ridge with $\lambda=10^{-3}$ stays at 0.037, and at degree 15 the same penalty shrinks the coefficient norm from 856 to 1.8. Panel (b) shows the cost of this: the training error goes up with $\lambda$. That is expected, since OLS is by construction the minimizer of the training error \cite[Eq.~(3.91)]{HjorthJensen2026}. We do not show the test $R^2$, because on a fixed test set it is simply $1-\MSE/\operatorname{var}(y)$.

The singular values explain why the penalty works so well. At degree 15 they range from 27.6 down to $1.5\times10^{-4}$. Ridge multiplies the component along each singular direction by $d_j^2/(d_j^2+n\lambda)$ \cite[Eqs.~(3.48) and (3.95)]{HjorthJensen2026}, so the directions with the smallest singular values are suppressed first, and those are the directions that carry the unstable wiggles of a high-degree fit. On the main split, a grid of 45 penalties gave its lowest test MSE, 0.0135, at degree 10 and $\lambda\approx2\times10^{-6}$, compared with 0.0189 for the best OLS fit. Since this choice was made on the test set, we only treat it as exploratory; the final model in part i is chosen by cross-validation.

Whether shrinkage helps depends on how well the data already constrain the fit. We compared OLS and Ridge on 40 paired data sets per setting. With 50 points, degree-20 Ridge with $\lambda=10^{-7}$ lowered the median test MSE from 0.236 to 0.018 and did better in 38 of the 40 runs. With 1000 points the two were almost equal (0.0105 for OLS and 0.0108 for Ridge), and Ridge did better in only 3 of 40 runs. Without noise, Ridge was clearly worse (0.0010 against $6\times10^{-5}$). In other words, shrinkage pays off when the variance dominates, and otherwise it mainly adds bias. Ridge hardly moves the optimal degree either. With 100 points, the median test MSE is lowest at degree 12 for OLS and at degree 13 for $\lambda=10^{-7}$, while $\lambda=10^{-3}$ makes the curve almost flat, between 0.020 and 0.022 for all degrees from 8 to 20. The main gain from Ridge in this problem is thus that a much wider range of degrees becomes safe to use.

\subsection{c) Bias, variance and bootstrap}\label{sec:res_biasvar}
% Evidence: part_c.json/train_test,n=100,n=1000 (incl. bias2_mc, variance_mc, error_mc); part_c_bias_variance.py; edge_effects.json/part_c.
\begin{figure}[tb]
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{c_train_test.pdf}

Thick curves are the mean and median over all 100 sets, and thin curves show 40 of the individual sets. Figure~\ref{fig:hastie} is our version of Fig.~2.11 in Ref.~\cite{Hastie2009}. We drew 100 independent training sets of 80 points and scored each fit on the same 2000 test points. The mean training error falls steadily, while the mean test error has its minimum at degree 8 (0.0166) and then rises. The median test error is lowest at degree 12 (0.0134). The difference comes from the occasional training set that gives a very poor high-degree fit and pulls the mean up.

With 800 training points, Fig.~\ref{fig:biasvar}(b) looks like the textbook picture of Eq.~(\ref{eq:biasvar}). The squared bias, measured against the known $f$, falls with degree, the variance grows slowly, and the bootstrap variance agrees with the variance over 400 fresh training sets ($1.79\times10^{-4}$ and $1.81\times10^{-4}$ at degree 12). If the bias is instead measured against the noisy $y$, the noise is included as well. The difference between the two versions is 0.0103, close to $\sigma^2=0.01$, as expected from Sec.~\ref{sec:biasvar_theory}. The fresh training sets also show where the optimum lies. With 80 training points their error is lowest at degree 8, close to where the squared bias (0.0047) and the variance (0.0060) cross. With 800 points the error is almost flat from degree 16 to 20 and lowest at the end of the grid.

With only 80 training points (panel a), the bootstrap is far from the fresh-set reference. It selects degree 4 instead of 8, and at degree 8 its variance is 7.75 rather than 0.006. Again the three extrapolated test points from part a are to blame. From degree 7 and up they produce more than 99.9\% of the bootstrap variance, and without them the degree-8 variance is 0.0044 and the bootstrap error is lowest at degree 8, in agreement with the reference. The reason lies in how the resamples are drawn. Each training point is missing from a resample with probability $(1-1/80)^{80}\approx0.37$, and a resample that loses the leftmost points has to extrapolate even further, which a high-degree polynomial amplifies. The bootstrap can only reuse the one training set we have, and that set covers the left edge poorly, whereas the fresh training sets draw new inputs every time.

\subsection{d) Cross-validation}\label{sec:res_cv}
% Evidence: part_d.json/ols,ridge,own_vs_sklearn,reshuffle,leakage; ridge_supplement.json/ensemble.
\begin{figure*}[tb]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{d_cross_validation.pdf}
\caption{Cross-validation errors against degree. Left: OLS with five and ten folds, with descriptive fold standard errors as bands. Right: ten-fold CV for Ridge. Both CV variants select degree 12 for OLS, while the bootstrap selects degree 4 on the original small sample. Scaling is fitted inside each fold. \end{figure*}

Five- and ten-fold cross-validation both select degree 12 for OLS, with CV MSE 0.020 and 0.017 (Fig.~\ref{fig:cv}). For Ridge, ten-fold CV picks degree 12 with the smallest penalty in the grid, $\lambda=10^{-10}$, which is effectively OLS, and five-fold CV picks degree 11 with $\lambda=10^{-6}$. Once the degree is chosen well, this data set does not seem to need any regularization.

CV and the small-sample bootstrap disagree (degree 12 against 4), but they do not measure quite the same thing. The bootstrap evaluates refits on the 20 fixed test points, including the three extrapolated ones, and each refit sees only about 63\% of the distinct training points. CV trains on 80--90\% of all 100 observations and uses every observation once for validation. Removing the three extrapolated points moves the bootstrap choice to degree 8 (part c), so the disagreement is caused by extrapolation and not by a different balance between bias and variance in the interior. Since the scaling is fitted inside each fold, nothing from the held-out fold reaches the fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling}). We also checked what leakage would have cost here. Standardizing all 100 points before splitting left the degree-12 OLS CV error unchanged, as expected from the invariance of OLS predictions to rescaling, and changed the Ridge CV error by less than 1\%. On $[-1,1]$, where all columns already have similar sizes, the effect is small, but on data with very different column scales it need not be.

The selected degree is itself uncertain. Over 20 different random fold assignments it ranged from 4 to 13 with five folds and from 10 to 13 with ten folds, with median 12. The one-standard-error rule \cite[Sec.~3.14]{HjorthJensen2026} also gives degree 12. What we can trust is the error level near the minimum, about 0.017, more than the exact degree.

\subsection{e) Gradient descent and automatic derivatives}\label{sec:res_gd}
% Evidence: part_e.json/gradient_check_max_rel,timing,degree6; part_a.json/scaling (Hessian spectra with and without standardization).
The analytical and automatic gradients agree to a relative $1.1\times10^{-15}$ for OLS and Ridge at degrees 6 and 15, i.e.\ to machine precision. Used inside gradient descent they give identical runs: 14\,399 iterations for degree-6 OLS, with final coefficients that differ by $4\times10^{-15}$. One AD gradient cost as much as 16 cost evaluations at $n=80$, but only 3.0 at $n=10^6$. The large-$n$ value is the small constant factor expected for reverse mode, while at small $n$ the overhead of the AD library dominates.

The learning rate is limited by the Hessian $H=\frac2nX^TX$. For degree-6 OLS its eigenvalues range from 7.70 down to 0.0037, so gradient descent is only stable for $\eta<2/h_{\max}=0.260$ \cite[Eq.~(4.20)]{HjorthJensen2026}. At the optimal rate $2/(h_{\max}+h_{\min})$ the error shrinks by a factor $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ per step, with $\kappa=2090$ \cite[Eq.~(4.26)]{HjorthJensen2026}. Reaching $r<10^{-6}$ should then take about $(\kappa/2)\ln 10^6\approx14\,400$ iterations, and we needed 14\,399. Rates above the bound diverged along the steepest direction, and smaller rates were slower. Standardization matters for this count. With centering only, the degree-6 Hessian has $\kappa\approx4600$, more than twice the standardized value. At degree 15, on the other hand, $\kappa$ is of order $10^{10}$ either way ($9\times10^{10}$ and $3\times10^{10}$, the squares of the centred and standardized condition numbers in part a), because the columns stay nearly collinear. Ridge with $\lambda=0.1$ adds $2\lambda$ to every eigenvalue, which lowers $\kappa$ to 39, and gradient descent then converges in 244 iterations. The penalty therefore makes the optimization easier as well.

All methods were run with the same data, the same starting point and the same grid of 28 learning rates, with at most $10^5$ iterations (Table~\ref{tab:opt}, Fig.~\ref{fig:optimizers}). Momentum was by far the fastest, with 656 iterations for OLS against 17\,361 for plain GD on the same grid and 14\,399 for GD at its optimal rate. The lecture notes explain why. With optimally tuned parameters, momentum needs a number of iterations proportional to $\sqrt\kappa$ instead of $\kappa$ \cite[Eq.~(4.33)]{HjorthJensen2026}, and $\sqrt{2090}\approx46$. Our $\beta=0.9$ is not the optimal value, so the speed-up we see, a factor of 22, is smaller than that. Momentum's best rate, 0.46, is also above the plain-GD bound of 0.26, which does not apply to the momentum iteration. With Ridge ($\lambda=10^{-3}$) every method needed fewer iterations, as expected from the smaller condition number.

Adam converged at all 28 rates, which makes it the safest choice when a good rate is not known, but it needed 1144 iterations. AdaGrad slowed down as its sum of squared gradients grew. RMSprop never reached the target with a constant rate. Its error stalled at a level proportional to the rate, from $4.5\times10^{-4}$ at $\eta=10^{-3}$ to $1.4\times10^{-2}$ at $\eta=0.03$. Near the minimum the scaled step $g_k/\sqrt{a_k}$ does not shrink, so the iterate keeps jumping by about $\eta$. With a decaying rate the error came down to $1.5\times10^{-5}$, which is still above the target.

The adaptive methods do not beat momentum here because they rescale each coordinate separately. In effect they act as a diagonal preconditioner \cite[Eq.~(4.57)]{HjorthJensen2026}, which helps when the Hessian is close to diagonal. For polynomial features it is far from diagonal, since neighbouring powers of $x$ are almost parallel, and the lecture notes show the same failure for a rotated quadratic bowl \cite[Sec.~4.8]{HjorthJensen2026}. At degree 10, where $\kappa$ is much larger, none of the five methods reached the target within 50\,000 iterations.

Autograd returns 0 as the derivative of $|\theta|$ at $\theta=0$. This is a valid subgradient, and our analytical gradient uses the same convention. We ran all five update rules for 40\,000 iterations on the degree-10 problem with $\lambda=10^{-3}$. Each method's rate was chosen from a fixed grid of eight values using only the training objective, and the result was compared with scikit-learn's coordinate-descent solution (Table~\ref{tab:lasso}). Adam came closest, with a relative coefficient error of $3\times10^{-5}$, followed by momentum ($3.5\times10^{-3}$). Plain GD and AdaGrad were still 16--19\% away. For plain GD this partly reflects the grid: its selected rate, 0.1, is the largest one and lies below the stability limit $2/h_{\max}\approx0.156$, so a rate closer to that limit would give a smaller error.

None of the gradient methods produced exact zeros, while the reference sets three coefficients exactly to zero. This is expected. A subgradient step moves a coefficient past zero rather than onto it, whereas coordinate descent uses the soft-thresholding rule \cite[Eq.~(3.63)]{HjorthJensen2026}, which returns exactly zero whenever the data term is weak enough. Runge's function is even, but our random sample is not symmetric, so the odd coefficients do not have to vanish.

Test error turned out to be a poor measure of convergence. After 10\,000 GD steps at degree 10, OLS had a relative coefficient error of 99\%, and still a lower test MSE (0.0199) than the exact OLS solution (0.0370). Stopping early acted as a regularizer on this split. The Adam fits for Ridge and Lasso matched the test MSEs of their references to four digits. Convergence should therefore be checked on the coefficients or on the objective, not on the test error.

\subsection{h) Stochastic gradient descent}\label{sec:res_sgd}
% Evidence: part_h.json/setup,batch_size,methods_M10; part_h_ridge.json.
\begin{figure*}[tb]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{h_sgd.pdf}
\caption{SGD for degree-6 OLS with 80 training observations, averaged over five shuffle seeds, with separately tuned rates. Left: different batch sizes with constant and decaying rates. Right: the five update rules at batch size 10. Decaying rates reduce the stochastic fluctuations but can slow down late progress. Curves are smoothed in logarithmic epoch bins, and the horizontal line is a reference scale, not a stopping criterion.}\label{fig:sgd}
\end{figure*}

We ran SGD on degree-6 OLS for 2000 epochs with batch sizes 1, 5, 20 and 80, tuned each rate on the training cost and averaged over five shufflings (Fig.~\ref{fig:sgd}). With a constant rate, SGD does not settle at the minimum. It fluctuates around it, because each mini-batch gradient is only a noisy estimate of the full gradient. The variance of these fluctuations is proportional to $\eta/M$ for batch size $M$ \cite[Eq.~(4.50)]{HjorthJensen2026}, so they shrink with a decreasing rate or with larger batches. The decaying schedule $\eta_t=\eta_0t_1/(t+t_1)$ reduced the final excess cost for batch size 5 almost tenfold, from $4.4\times10^{-4}$ to $4.7\times10^{-5}$. The batch size also changes the stable rate. A single observation has the Hessian $2x_ix_i^T$, whose largest eigenvalue $2\|x_i\|^2$ is up to 127 here. Applying Eq.~(4.20) of the lecture notes to it gives $\eta<0.016$ for batch size 1, compared with 0.26 for the full batch. The tuned rates, 0.004 and 0.25, respect both limits.

Mini-batches did not save any time here. One epoch of SGD costs the same number of operations as one full gradient step \cite[Eq.~(4.38)]{HjorthJensen2026}, but with batch size 1 it consists of 80 small updates, each with its own Python overhead. As a result, 2000 epochs took 0.81~s with batch size 1 and 0.044~s with the full batch. SGD pays off for large data sets, where a single full gradient is expensive, but not for 80 observations.

Among the update rules at batch size 10, Adam reached the lowest coefficient error, 0.020 after 2000 epochs, but it took about twice as long as plain SGD (0.21 against 0.11~s).

For Ridge with $\lambda=10^{-3}$ we compared the same kind of runs with the closed-form Ridge solution from part b. The five update rules ended at relative coefficient errors between 0.0185 (Adam) and 0.0510 (momentum), and plain SGD and full-batch GD at the optimal step both reached 0.0485. This agreement has a simple explanation. The flattest direction of the Ridge Hessian carries 93\% of the initial error, and the error along it shrinks by the factor $(1-\eta h_{\min})^k$ after $k$ updates \cite[Eq.~(4.19)]{HjorthJensen2026}. The product $\eta k$ is about 500 in both runs, which predicts $r=0.0485$ for full-batch GD and 0.052 for SGD. The same direction explains why the training cost and the coefficient error can rank the methods differently, since an error along a flat direction barely raises the cost. Full-batch GD took 0.016~s, against 0.11--0.21~s for the mini-batch runs. For a problem this small, the SVD solution is both exact and the cheapest, for OLS as well as for Ridge. Among the iterative methods, the best choice depends on the gradient. With exact full-batch gradients, momentum reached the tolerance fastest (part f). With noisy mini-batch gradients it ended with the largest Ridge error, and Adam came closest to the closed-form solution for both OLS and Ridge, as it did for the non-smooth Lasso cost in part g.

For the final comparison we used ten-fold CV over degrees 1--20, with 21 Ridge penalties ($10^{-10}$ to 1) and seven Lasso penalties ($10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$). The selected models were refitted on all 100 points and only then evaluated on 2000 new observations (seed 2027), so the test set played no part in the selection.

At $\sigma=0.1$, degree-12 OLS and Ridge with a negligible penalty give the same CV score, $0.0173\pm0.0014$, and the same test MSE, 0.0113 (Table~\ref{tab:final}). We prefer OLS, since it has one tuning parameter less. Lasso is slightly worse, and its selected penalty lies at the edge of the grid, so a smaller penalty might still improve it. At $\sigma=0.3$ the selected degrees drop to 8--10 and Ridge has the lowest CV score, but the three methods differ by much less than the fold uncertainty.

The CV error, 0.0173, is clearly higher than the test error, 0.0113. Most of this gap comes from the noise in our particular sample: the 100 observations have a realized noise variance of 0.0139, against 0.0101 for the 2000 new points. Measured against the true function, the errors are small in both cases, 0.0023 for the fold fits and 0.0016 for the refit. The selected model is thus within about 0.002 of $f$ in mean square, and the rest of the error is noise.

To see which term in Eq.~(\ref{eq:biasvar}) each method reduces, we drew 150 independent training sets of 80 points and fitted degree-15 models to each. For OLS, the squared bias was 0.0065 and the variance 0.21. Ridge and Lasso with $\lambda=3.16\times10^{-4}$ reduced the variance about sixtyfold, to 0.0035, while the squared bias stayed close to the OLS value (0.0067 and 0.0049). The penalties work on the variance, and so does choosing a lower degree, while the noise term $\sigma^2$ cannot be reduced by any model. That the bias for Lasso comes out even lower than for OLS is not significant. With a variance of 0.21, averaging only 150 fits inflates the OLS bias estimate by about $0.21/150\approx0.0014$.

All 3080 Lasso fits behind Table~\ref{tab:final} passed the convergence checks described in Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}. With only 100 observations, fold-to-fold variation and finite grids, the table cannot support a strong general ranking of the three methods. On this problem the differences between them are smaller than the uncertainty.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
Ten-fold cross-validation selected a degree-12 OLS fit. On 2000 independent observations its MSE was 0.0113, close to the noise variance, and its mean-square error relative to the true function was only 0.0016. Ridge with an almost vanishing penalty gave the same result. The high-degree fits were accurate where the data pinned them down and failed where they had to extrapolate, much as Runge's analysis of polynomial interpolation would lead one to expect. A few test inputs beyond the outermost training point caused the spikes in the single-split test error and nearly all of the small-sample bootstrap variance.

Ridge removes this instability by damping the directions with small singular values. At degree 15 it reduced the variance by a factor of about 60 at almost no cost in bias, and at degree 6 it lowered the condition number seen by gradient descent from 2090 to 39. Its price, a bias towards smaller coefficients, only mattered when the data already constrained the fit well. Lasso can give sparse solutions, but it has no closed form, and our gradient solvers never produced exact zeros. On this problem it was not clearly better than OLS or Ridge. Among the optimizers, momentum was fastest with exact full-batch gradients, while Adam was the least sensitive to the learning rate and came closest to the reference with mini-batch gradients and for Lasso. RMSprop with a constant rate stalled at an error floor proportional to the rate, and mini-batches did not pay off for 80 observations.

The results have clear limitations. They rest on a single random design of 100 points, and a single 20-point test split turned out to be unreliable, since three extrapolated inputs dominated its ranking of the degrees. The bootstrap is conditional on one training set and inherited its poor coverage of the left edge, and the degree selected by five-fold CV varied from 4 to 13 between fold assignments. The optimizer rankings only hold for the rate grids, tolerance and iteration budgets we used, and the Lasso penalty grid ended at the selected value. Finally, our bias and variance estimates rely on knowing $f$. For real data only the noisy-target version, which includes $\sigma^2$, can be computed.

Two extensions would be natural next steps. Repeating the analysis with Chebyshev-distributed inputs, which cluster near $\pm1$, or with test inputs restricted to the training range, would separate interpolation from extrapolation and give a quantitative test of the edge explanation. Nested cross-validation would show how stable the selected model is from sample to sample. Much of the pipeline carries over directly to the neural-network regression in Project~2: scaling fitted inside each fold, a learning rate set from the largest Hessian eigenvalue, and a decaying SGD schedule. The main difference is that there will be no closed-form solution to check the optimizers against.
