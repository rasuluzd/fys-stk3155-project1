\title{Regression, resampling and gradient descent for the Runge function}
\author{Rasul Ruslanovitsj {\O}zdber}
\affiliation{Department of Physics, University of Oslo, Norway}
\date{October 5, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Polynomial regression is the simplest setting in which model complexity, regularization and the reliability of error estimates can be studied exactly. We fitted Runge's function, the classic example of polynomial instability near the ends of an interval, from 100 noisy observations, and asked when regularization helps and how reliably resampling and gradient methods identify a suitable model. We implemented ordinary least squares, Ridge, the bootstrap and plain, momentum, adaptive and stochastic gradient descent, and used scikit-learn for cross-validation and reference fits and Autograd for automatic derivatives. Ten-fold cross-validation selected a degree-12 OLS fit with MSE $0.0173\pm0.0014$; on 2000 independent observations its MSE was 0.0113, close to the noise variance 0.01, and nearly unregularized Ridge performed identically. High-degree fits failed mainly where they extrapolated beyond the outermost training points, and these few points dominated single-split test errors and small-sample bootstrap variances; Ridge suppressed this instability. Momentum reached a fixed coefficient tolerance fastest, and Adam was the least sensitive to the learning rate. Complexity should therefore be chosen by cross-validated error, and optimizers compared at a stated accuracy and cost.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
\emph{The abstract and this section contain LLM-assisted text (level 3); see Appendix~\ref{app:llm}.}

Fitting a function to noisy observations is the basic task of supervised learning, and polynomial regression is its simplest instance. The model is linear in its parameters, so ordinary least squares (OLS) and Ridge regression have closed-form solutions against which every other method can be checked \cite{HjorthJensen2026,Hastie2009}. Runge's function $f(x)=1/(1+25x^2)$ on $[-1,1]$ is the classic test case: Runge showed that interpolating it with polynomials at equally spaced points diverges near the ends of the interval as the degree grows \cite{Runge1901}. With noisy, randomly placed data the same sensitivity appears as variance. A high-degree polynomial is pinned down only by the few observations near $\pm1$, and where it must extrapolate it can swing far from the data. Because $f$ is known, the bias and the variance of a fit can be measured directly rather than only estimated, which makes the problem a laboratory for the bias--variance trade-off that governs every later model in the course.

Three groups of methods address this trade-off. Ridge \cite{Hoerl1970} and Lasso \cite{Tibshirani1996} regression penalize large coefficients and exchange variance for bias. The bootstrap \cite{Efron1979} and $k$-fold cross-validation \cite{Hastie2009} estimate the test error without new data and are used to select the degree and the penalty. Gradient descent replaces the closed-form solutions, with momentum \cite{Polyak1964}, the adaptive rules AdaGrad \cite{Duchi2011}, RMSprop \cite{Tieleman2012} and Adam \cite{Kingma2015}, and stochastic mini-batches \cite{Robbins1951}. It is the optimizer of the neural networks in the next project, where no closed form exists.

Following Project~1 \cite{Project1_2026}, we asked four questions: how polynomial degree, sample size and noise set the trade-off for OLS; when Ridge and Lasso improve on OLS; whether the bootstrap and cross-validation select the same complexity; and how efficiently the gradient methods recover the closed-form solutions. We implemented OLS and Ridge through the singular value decomposition, the bootstrap, the gradient-based optimizers and the subgradient Lasso ourselves. Scikit-learn supplied cross-validation and reference fits \cite{Pedregosa2011}, and Autograd supplied automatic derivatives \cite{Maclaurin2015}.

Section~\ref{sec:theory} presents the models, the bias--variance decomposition and the optimizers. Section~\ref{sec:implementation} describes the implementation and its tests against closed-form and library results. Section~\ref{sec:results} answers parts a--i, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} summarizes the findings, their limitations and possible extensions. The code, settings and saved results are at \url{https://github.com/rasuluzd/fys-stk3155-project1}.

\subsection{Data, scaling and error measures}\label{sec:data}\label{sec:scaling}
Following part a, we generated
\begin{equation}
y_i=f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i,\quad f(x)=\frac{1}{1+25x^2},\quad
\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).
\end{equation}
Unless specified otherwise, 100 inputs were drawn uniformly from $[-1,1]$, with $\sigma=0.1$, seed 2026 and an 80/20 training/test split. We fitted polynomials through degree 15 and extended the sensitivity and model-selection studies to degree 20.

For degree $p$, the raw features are $A_{ij}=x_i^j$, $j=1,\ldots,p$. Using only the training observations, we computed column means $\mu_j$, standard deviations $s_j$ and target mean $\bar y$. The fitted prediction is
\begin{equation}
\widehat y(x)=\bar y+\sum_{j=1}^p\theta_j\frac{x^j-\mu_j}{s_j}.
\end{equation}
% Evidence: part_a.json/scaling (column standard deviations); benchmarks.json (scaled vs centred OLS).
Centering removes the intercept from the fit, so it is never penalized; the raw intercept is $\bar y-\sum_j\theta_j\mu_j/s_j$ \cite[Sec.~3.13]{HjorthJensen2026}. Standardization matters less here than for most data: on $[-1,1]$ every monomial is bounded by one, and at degree 15 the column standard deviations lie between 0.11 and 0.50. OLS predictions are in fact invariant to rescaling the columns; with and without standardization they agreed to $1.3\times10^{-13}$. Scaling matters for two other reasons. The Ridge and Lasso penalties act on coefficient sizes, which become comparable only after standardization, and the stable step size of gradient descent is set by the spectrum of $X^TX$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:gd_theory}). Means and scales are computed from the training observations only and then applied to the test data. They are refitted inside every bootstrap sample and CV fold, because statistics that include held-out points would leak information about them into the fit. Scaling cannot remove the strong correlations between neighbouring powers of $x$, so the design stays ill-conditioned at high degree.

On each evaluation set we used
\begin{equation}
\MSE=\frac1n\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2,\qquad
R^2=1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}.
\end{equation}
Here $\bar y$ is the mean of that evaluation set. Negative $R^2$ means predictions are worse than its constant mean \cite[Sec.~3.5]{HjorthJensen2026}.

\subsection{OLS, Ridge and Lasso}
Write $X$ for the standardized training features, $y$ for the centered target and $n$ for the training size. The three costs are
\begin{align}
C_{\rm OLS}&=\frac1n\|y-X\theta\|^2,\nonumber\\
C_{\rm Ridge}&=C_{\rm OLS}+\lambda\|\theta\|_2^2,\nonumber\\
C_{\rm Lasso}&=C_{\rm OLS}+\lambda\|\theta\|_1.
\end{align}
The OLS gradient $-\frac2nX^T(y-X\theta)$ vanishes when $X^TX\theta=X^Ty$. Ridge adds $2\lambda\theta$ to the gradient and hence $n\lambda I$ to $X^TX$ \cite{Hoerl1970}. The solutions are
\begin{align}
\widehat\theta_{\rm OLS}&=X^+y,\nonumber\\
\widehat\theta_{\rm Ridge}&=(X^TX+n\lambda I)^{-1}X^Ty.
\end{align}
We evaluated these using the pseudoinverse/SVD, without explicitly inverting $X^TX$. For $X=UDV^T$, Ridge multiplies each OLS singular-direction coefficient by
\begin{equation}
q_j=\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+n\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Small singular values are damped most strongly, reducing variance at the cost of bias \cite[Secs.~3.3 and 3.8]{HjorthJensen2026}. Lasso can set slopes exactly to zero, but has no general closed form \cite{Tibshirani1996}, \cite[Sec.~3.9]{HjorthJensen2026}. Our mean-loss convention requires scikit-learn parameters $\alpha=n\lambda$ for Ridge and $\alpha=\lambda/2$ for Lasso.

A Lasso solution is characterized by the stationarity conditions $g_j+\lambda\operatorname{sign}(\theta_j)=0$ for nonzero slopes and $|g_j|\leq\lambda$ for zero slopes, where $g$ is the gradient of the data term \cite[Sec.~3.9]{HjorthJensen2026}. We used scikit-learn's coordinate-descent fits as references only when these conditions and the duality gap were satisfied to $10^{-7}$ \cite{SklearnLasso}.

\subsection{Bias--variance decomposition and resampling}\label{sec:biasvar_theory}\label{sec:resampling}
Let $g_D(x)$ be the fit obtained from a random training set $D$, so that $\tilde y=g_D(x)$ in the notation of the project, and let $m(x)=\E_D[g_D(x)]$ be the mean fit. At a fixed input $x$ a new observation is $Y=f(x)+\varepsilon$, with $\E[\varepsilon]=0$, $\operatorname{var}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2$ and $\varepsilon$ independent of $D$. Adding and subtracting $m$ gives
\[
Y-g_D=(f-m)+(m-g_D)+\varepsilon,
\]
and squaring gives
\begin{align}
(Y-g_D)^2={}&(f-m)^2+(m-g_D)^2+\varepsilon^2\nonumber\\
&+2(f-m)(m-g_D)+2(f-m)\varepsilon\nonumber\\
&+2(m-g_D)\varepsilon .
\end{align}
We take the expectation over both $D$ and $\varepsilon$. Since $f-m$ does not depend on $D$, the first cross term averages to $2(f-m)\,\E_D[m-g_D]=0$ by the definition of $m$. The second vanishes because $\E[\varepsilon]=0$. The third vanishes because $\varepsilon$ is independent of $D$, so $\E[(m-g_D)\varepsilon]=\E_D[m-g_D]\,\E[\varepsilon]=0$. Hence
\begin{equation}
\E_{D,\varepsilon}[(Y-g_D)^2]
=\underbrace{(f-m)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
+\underbrace{\E_D[(g_D-m)^2]}_{\text{variance}}+\sigma^2.
\label{eq:biasvar}
\end{equation}
Averaging each term over the evaluation inputs gives the decomposition $\E[(y-\tilde y)^2]=\mathrm{Bias}^2[\tilde y]+\operatorname{var}[\tilde y]+\sigma^2$ of the project, Eq.~(2.52) of the lecture notes \cite[Sec.~2.10]{HjorthJensen2026}. The squared bias is the systematic error of the mean fit, the variance measures how much the fit changes from one training set to another, and $\sigma^2$ is the noise in the new observation, which no model can remove. The expectation $\E_D$ is over training sets, that is, over both the random input locations and the training noise.

The bootstrap estimates training-set variation by resampling paired training observations with replacement \cite{Efron1979}. A resample contains on average only $1-(1-1/n)^n\approx63\%$ of the distinct observations, so each observation is missing from about 37\% of the resamples. Each resample is fitted and predicted on the same fixed test inputs \cite[Sec.~2.11]{HjorthJensen2026}, following week~36, Exercises~2--3 \cite{Exercises36}. With $B$ predictions $g_{bi}$ and their mean $\bar g_i$, the stored arrays obey
\begin{align}
\frac1{n_t B}\sum_{i,b}(y_i-g_{bi})^2
={}&\frac1{n_t}\sum_i(y_i-\bar g_i)^2\nonumber\\
&+\frac1{n_t B}\sum_{i,b}(g_{bi}-\bar g_i)^2.
\end{align}
The first term uses noisy $y$, so it includes the noise contribution in expectation. Its difference from the same quantity using known $f$ is
\[
\frac1{n_t}\sum_i\left[\varepsilon_i^2+2\varepsilon_i(f_i-\bar g_i)\right].
\]
This averages to $\sigma^2$ over independent test noise, but need not equal it for one fixed test set. We show both versions.

In $k$-fold CV, each fold is predicted by a model fitted to the other $k-1$ folds \cite[Ch.~7]{Hastie2009}. We used shuffled five- and ten-fold splits, identical across candidates, and selected the lowest mean validation MSE \cite[Secs.~2.12 and 3.14]{HjorthJensen2026}. Fold standard errors are descriptive because training folds overlap.

\subsection{Gradient methods}\label{sec:gd_theory}
The analytical gradients and Ridge Hessian are
\begin{align}
\nabla C_{\rm OLS}&=\frac2nX^T(X\theta-y),\nonumber\\
\nabla C_{\rm Ridge}&=\nabla C_{\rm OLS}+2\lambda\theta,\nonumber\\
H&=\frac2nX^TX+2\lambda I.
\end{align}
Plain GD updates $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\nabla C(\theta_k)$. Each quadratic error component is multiplied by $1-\eta h_j$, so convergence requires $0<\eta<2/h_{\max}$ on a positive-definite problem. The optimal constant step size is $2/(h_{\max}+h_{\min})$ \cite[Secs.~4.3--4.5]{HjorthJensen2026}; week~37, Exercise~4 connects this bound to GD and AD \cite{Exercises37}.

Write $g_k=\nabla C(\theta_{k-1})$ and subtract the step $d_k$ from $\theta_{k-1}$. The implemented updates are
\begin{align}
\text{Momentum:}\quad d_k&=\beta d_{k-1}+\eta g_k,\nonumber\\
\text{AdaGrad:}\quad a_k&=a_{k-1}+g_k^2,\nonumber\\
\text{RMSprop:}\quad a_k&=\rho a_{k-1}+(1-\rho)g_k^2.
\end{align}
Both adaptive steps are $d_k=\eta g_k/(\sqrt{a_k}+\epsilon)$. AdaGrad's denominator keeps growing; RMSprop forgets old squared gradients. Adam instead uses
\begin{align}
m_k&=\beta_1m_{k-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_k,\nonumber\\
v_k&=\beta_2v_{k-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_k^2,\nonumber\\
d_k&=\eta\frac{m_k/(1-\beta_1^k)}{\sqrt{v_k/(1-\beta_2^k)}+\epsilon}.
\end{align}
All operations are componentwise, state starts at zero and $k=1$ on the first update. Momentum \cite{Polyak1964} retains the previous step; AdaGrad \cite{Duchi2011} and RMSprop \cite{Tieleman2012} rescale each coordinate by its gradient history; Adam \cite{Kingma2015} combines both ideas and corrects its initially small moving averages. We used $\beta=0.9$, $\rho=0.99$, $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.999)$ and $\epsilon=10^{-8}$, outside the square root, as in week~38's Tuesday implementation \cite[Secs.~4.6 and 4.8--4.11]{HjorthJensen2026}. The comparisons follow week~37, Exercise~6 and week~38, Exercises~1--3 \cite{Exercises37,Exercises38,Week38Tuesday}.

Autograd differentiates the implemented scalar cost by applying the chain rule to the elementary operations recorded while the cost is evaluated \cite{Maclaurin2015,Baydin2018}. This is neither symbolic differentiation, which manipulates expressions and can produce very large formulas, nor numerical differentiation, which perturbs the inputs and suffers from truncation and round-off error: AD gives the exact derivative of the program, up to floating-point rounding. In reverse mode one forward and one backward sweep give all $p$ partial derivatives at a cost of a small constant multiple of one cost evaluation, independent of $p$ \cite{Baydin2018}, \cite[Sec.~4.14]{HjorthJensen2026}.

For Lasso we add $\lambda\operatorname{sign}(\theta)$ to the OLS gradient and choose $\operatorname{sign}(0)=0$. This is a valid subgradient because the subdifferential of $|\theta|$ at zero is $[-1,1]$; the value 1 that JAX returns in the project notebook is an equally valid choice \cite{Project1_2026}. A fixed-step subgradient method does not, however, produce exact zeros at a finite iteration.

SGD \cite{Robbins1951} reshuffles the training observations each epoch and applies the same update rules to mini-batch gradients, dividing the data term by the actual batch size. Optimizer state persists between batches. We compared constant rates with $\eta_t=\eta_0t_1/(t+t_1)$, where $t$ counts updates from zero \cite[Sec.~4.7]{HjorthJensen2026}, as in week~38, Exercise~4 and week~39's SGD examples \cite{Exercises38,HjorthJensen2026week39}.

\subsection{a) Ordinary least squares}\label{sec:res_ols}
% Evidence: part_a.json/main, scaling, ensembles; part_a_ols.py; edge_effects.json/main_split, part_a.
On the main split, test MSE was 0.01894 at degree 12 and 0.01899 at degree 6 (Fig.~\ref{fig:mse_r2}). This difference is too small to establish a unique optimum from 20 test points. Training error continued to fall, while high-degree test error increased. The corresponding fall in test $R^2$ shows that fitting training noise does not improve prediction.

Figure~\ref{fig:edge} shows where the high-degree fits fail. Three of the 20 test inputs lie below the smallest training input, $x=-0.953$, so every model must extrapolate there. These three points carry 89\% and 92\% of the test error at degrees 13 and 15; without them the test MSE stays between 0.014 and 0.018 from degree 8 to 15. The spikes in Fig.~\ref{fig:mse_r2} are therefore the Runge phenomenon at the left edge, where the two outermost training inputs are $-0.953$ and $-0.883$, rather than a uniform loss of accuracy.

The standardized coefficient norm rose from 2.70 at degree 6 to 856 at degree 15 (Fig.~\ref{fig:coef}). Large coefficients cancel to form the fitted curve, making it sensitive to data perturbations. At degree 15, standardization reduced the design condition number from $4.7\times10^5$ to $1.9\times10^5$, but the columns remained strongly correlated. This explains why scaling alone did not cure the instability.

Over 200 datasets per setting, the degree giving the lowest median test MSE increased from 10 to 18 when total sample size increased from 50 to 1000 at $\sigma=0.1$. At $N=100$, increasing noise from 0.05 to 0.3 moved it from degree 16 to 8. More data supported greater complexity; more noise favored simpler fits. These are median results: rare unstable fits can make the mean much larger.

\subsection{b) Ridge regression}\label{sec:res_ridge}
% Evidence: part_b.json/main, svd_degree15; ridge_supplement.json/main, ensemble.
Ridge used degrees 1--20 and 45 penalties from $10^{-10}$ to 10. The main-split minimum test MSE was 0.01347 at degree 10 and $\lambda=1.78\times10^{-6}$, below OLS's 0.01894. This is an exploratory grid minimum; part i uses CV and a separate test set for final selection.

Figures~\ref{fig:ridge_metrics} and \ref{fig:ridge_coefficients} repeat the scores and coefficient analysis at fixed penalties. Increasing $\lambda$ limits the large coefficients, but excessive shrinkage increases both training and test error. At degree 15, singular values ranged from 27.59 to $1.49\times10^{-4}$. The factors $q_j$ suppress these small, unstable directions most strongly, explaining Ridge's wider usable degree range.

At $N=1000$, the values were 0.01049 for OLS and 0.01083 for Ridge, with Ridge improving only 3 of 40 runs. At $N=100$ without noise, Ridge gave 0.00101 against OLS's $6.17\times10^{-5}$. Shrinkage helps when variance dominates, but can add unnecessary bias when the data already constrain the fit.

\subsection{c) Bias, variance and bootstrap}\label{sec:res_biasvar}
% Evidence: part_c.json/train_test,n=100,n=1000; part_c_bias_variance.py; edge_effects.json/part_c.
Figure~\ref{fig:hastie} compares 100 independent training sets of 80 observations, scored on 2000 fixed noisy test observations. Mean test MSE was lowest at degree 8, at 0.01656; median MSE favored degree 12, at 0.01339. The difference reflects occasional very inaccurate high-degree fits. The average training error fell throughout.

The bootstrap used 200 resamples per degree, with a reference of 400 independently generated training sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:biasvar}). With 80 training points, bootstrap error selected degree 4 and the fresh-set reference selected degree 8. At degree 8, variance estimates were 7.754 and 0.00597. The cause is the three extrapolated test inputs of part a: from degree 7 upward they account for more than 99.9\% of the bootstrap variance, and without them the degree-8 variance is 0.0044, comparable to the fresh-set value. Without these points the bootstrap error is also lowest at degree 8, the degree selected by the fresh-set reference. Each training observation is absent from a resample with probability $(1-1/80)^{80}\approx0.37$, and a resample that loses the leftmost training point must extrapolate over a longer interval, which a high-degree polynomial amplifies. The bootstrap is conditional on one training set with poor coverage of the left edge, whereas the fresh sets redraw the input locations; 400 fresh sets also cannot precisely characterize rare extreme fits.

With 800 training points, the degree-12 variance estimates were much closer: $1.79\times10^{-4}$ and $1.81\times10^{-4}$. More observations reduced the variance, allowing higher degree. Using noisy $y$ instead of known $f$ raised the measured squared bias by 0.01030, close to $\sigma^2=0.01$. The finite-sample noise cross term explains the remaining difference. The curves illustrate the bias--variance trade-off, but small-sample resampling need not locate it accurately.

\subsection{d) Cross-validation}\label{sec:res_cv}
% Evidence: part_d.json/ols,ridge,own_vs_sklearn,reshuffle.
Five- and ten-fold CV both selected degree-12 OLS, with MSE 0.02016 and 0.01731. Ridge's 21-penalty grid, $10^{-10}$--1, selected degree 11 and $\lambda=10^{-6}$ for five folds, and degree 12 with $\lambda=10^{-10}$ for ten folds. The latter was essentially OLS.

Figure~\ref{fig:cv} shows that CV and the small-sample bootstrap did not select the same degree. They estimate the error on different points: the bootstrap scores refits built from about 63\% of the distinct training points on the 20 fixed test inputs, including the three extrapolated ones, whereas CV trains on 80--90\% of all 100 observations and scores every observation once. Without the three extrapolated inputs the bootstrap selects degree 8 instead of 4 (part c), so the disagreement comes from the extrapolation rather than from a different bias--variance balance in the interior. Neither estimate is exact. Across 20 shuffled fold assignments, the selected OLS degree ranged from 4--13 for five folds and 10--13 for ten folds, with median 12. The one-standard-error check also selected degree 12 on the original folds. This variation argues for reporting uncertainty alongside the selected degree.

\subsection{e) Gradient descent and automatic derivatives}\label{sec:res_gd}
% Evidence: part_e.json/gradient_check,timing,degree6.
For OLS and Ridge at degrees 6 and 15, analytical and AD gradients differed by at most $1.18\times10^{-15}$ relatively over 100 random coefficient vectors. Used inside gradient descent for degree-6 OLS, both derivative sources needed the same 14\,399 iterations at the Hessian-based rate, and their final vectors differed by at most $4.44\times10^{-15}$. AD cost 16.1 objective evaluations at $n=80$, falling to 2.81 at $n=10^6$. The large-$n$ value is the constant factor expected for reverse mode; at small $n$ the Python bookkeeping of the AD library dominates the array work.

For degree-6 OLS, $h_{\max}=7.69648$ and $h_{\min}=0.00368262$, giving $\eta<0.259859$. The rate $\eta_*=0.259735$ reached the target in 14\,399 iterations, exactly as predicted by the quadratic error recurrence. Smaller rates progressed slowly; rates above the bound amplified the steepest direction. Ridge with $\lambda=0.1$ reduced the condition number from 2090 to 38.8 and required 244 iterations. Regularization improved conditioning as well as changing the statistical fit.

\subsection{f) Momentum and adaptive updates}\label{sec:res_adaptive}
% Evidence: part_f.json/degree6,ridge_degree6,rmsprop_plateau,rmsprop_decay,degree10.

Table~\ref{tab:opt} compares iterations to $r<10^{-6}$. Momentum was fastest on this grid; Adam succeeded over the widest tested rate range. GD's grid omits $\eta_*$, explaining its larger count than in part e. AdaGrad slowed as its accumulated denominator grew.

Constant-rate RMSprop did not reach the target at any rate. Its relative error stalled at a floor proportional to the rate, from $4.5\times10^{-4}$ at $\eta=10^{-3}$ to $1.4\times10^{-2}$ at $\eta=0.03$. Near the minimum the normalized step $g_k/\sqrt{a_k}$ stays of order one, so the iterate keeps moving by about $\eta$ instead of settling. A decaying rate reduced the error to $1.50\times10^{-5}$ after 30\,000 steps, still above the target (Fig.~\ref{fig:optimizers}). At degree 10, none of the five methods reached the target within 50\,000 steps on the separate tested grid. Adaptive coordinate scaling did not remove the strong correlations between polynomial features.

Each run used 40\,000 updates and eight fixed candidate rates: seven logarithmic values from $10^{-4}$ to 0.1, plus $1/L$. Rates minimized mean training objective gap over the last 200 updates; test error did not select them.

Table~\ref{tab:lasso} compares final iterates with coordinate-descent Lasso at $\alpha=\lambda/2$. Adam was closest, followed by momentum. None produced exact zeros, while the reference had three: a small coefficient and an exact zero are different numerical outcomes. \subsection{h) Stochastic gradient descent}\label{sec:res_sgd}
% Evidence: part_h.json/setup,batch_size,methods_M10.
We ran degree-6 OLS for 2000 epochs with batch sizes 1, 5, 20 and 80. Rates were tuned on the last 200 epochs' excess training cost; curves summarize five shuffle seeds. For decay, $t_1$ corresponds to 250 epochs. Constant-rate SGD fluctuated near the solution because individual batch gradients need not vanish there. Decay reduced batch-5 final excess cost from $4.37\times10^{-4}$ to $4.70\times10^{-5}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:sgd}), but a rapidly shrinking step also slowed late coefficient convergence.

An epoch processes all 80 observations. Its main gradient work is proportional to $np$, like one full GD step, but batch size 1 requires 80 updates per epoch. Across 2000 epochs, batch sizes 1 and 80 took 0.871 and 0.0454~s in the recorded runs. Smaller updates therefore did not give a wall-time advantage on these small matrices.

For batch size 10, Adam's OLS coefficient error was 0.0204 after 2000 epochs, the lowest of the five update rules, at about twice the run time of plain SGD (0.23 versus 0.12~s). These timings describe the recorded machine and include update overhead. For this small regression problem, an SVD solve is simpler than tuning SGD; momentum is a useful iterative choice when high coefficient accuracy is required.

\subsection{i) Final model selection}\label{sec:res_final}
% Evidence: part_i.json/grids,sigma=0.1,sigma=0.3,bias_variance_degree15,lasso_convergence_cv; edge_effects.json/part_i.
Ten-fold CV used degrees 1--20, 21 Ridge penalties from $10^{-10}$ to 1, and seven Lasso penalties from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$. Each selected model was refitted on all 100 observations and evaluated on 2000 independent observations generated with seed 2027. The test set was used only after selection.

Table~\ref{tab:final} favors degree-12 OLS at $\sigma=0.1$. Ridge's CV score differs by only $1.8\times10^{-6}$, far below the descriptive fold standard error; its selected penalty is practically zero. OLS is therefore a reasonable choice without an additional tuning parameter. At $\sigma=0.3$, Ridge has the smallest CV MSE, but the three models' differences are again small compared with fold uncertainty. Increasing noise favors lower selected degrees.

The CV MSE of the selected OLS fit, 0.0173, lies well above its independent test MSE, 0.0113. The difference is mostly noise. The realized noise variance of the 100 observations is 0.0139, against 0.0101 for the 2000 new ones, while the error relative to $f$ is small in both cases: 0.0023 for the fold fits and 0.0016 for the refit. Cross-validation thus measures the error on the noisy observations of this particular sample; the selected model itself lies within about 0.002 of the true function in mean square.

Regularization mainly reduced variance here. In 150 independent degree-15 fits with 80 observations, OLS squared bias and variance were 0.00654 and 0.21098. At $\lambda=3.16\times10^{-4}$, Ridge gave 0.00670 and 0.00349, while Lasso gave 0.00485 and 0.00345. The penalties thus attack the variance term of Eq.~(\ref{eq:biasvar}), by a factor of about 60, at almost no cost in bias. The OLS bias is not reliably larger than Lasso's: it is estimated from the mean of only 150 fits, and with a variance of 0.211 this sampling error alone inflates it by about $0.211/150\approx0.0014$. These known-function calculations explain the effect of the penalties; they did not select the models in the table. Degree selection controls OLS complexity; Ridge damps unstable directions; Lasso also removes coefficients. None changes the observation noise.

All 3080 Lasso fits behind Table~\ref{tab:final} passed the convergence checks of Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}. At $\sigma=0.1$, however, Lasso selected the weakest tested penalty, so a search below $10^{-4}$ could still improve its score. With 100 observations, fold-to-fold variability and finite grids, the table supports no strong general ranking of the three methods.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
\emph{LLM-assisted text (level 3); see Appendix~\ref{app:llm}.}

Ten-fold cross-validation selected a degree-12 OLS fit whose MSE on 2000 independent observations was 0.0113, close to the noise variance; relative to the true function its mean-square error was only 0.0016, and nearly unregularized Ridge gave the same result. High-degree fits were accurate where the data constrained them and failed where they had to extrapolate, as Runge's analysis of polynomial interpolation leads one to expect. A few test inputs beyond the outermost training point produced the spikes in the single-split test error and essentially all of the small-sample bootstrap variance. Ridge removes this instability by damping the directions with small singular values. At degree 15 it reduced the variance by a factor of about 60 at almost no cost in bias, and at degree 6 it lowered the condition number seen by gradient descent from 2090 to 39. Its price, a bias towards smaller coefficients, mattered only when the data already constrained the fit. Among the optimizers, momentum reached the closed-form solution fastest and Adam was the least sensitive to the learning rate. Constant-rate RMSprop stalled at an error floor proportional to the rate, and mini-batches did not pay off for 80 observations.

These results have clear limitations. They rest on one random design of 100 points, and a single 20-point test split is unreliable: three extrapolated inputs dominated its ranking of the degrees. The bootstrap is conditional on one training set and inherited its poor coverage of the left edge, and the degree selected by five-fold CV varied from 4 to 13 between fold assignments. The optimizer rankings hold only for the stated rate grids, tolerance and budgets, and the Lasso penalty grid ended at the selected value. Our bias and variance estimates use the known $f$; on real data only the noisy-target version, which absorbs $\sigma^2$, can be computed.

Two extensions follow directly. Repeating the analysis with Chebyshev-distributed inputs, which cluster near $\pm1$, or with test inputs restricted to the training range would separate interpolation from extrapolation and test the edge interpretation quantitatively, and nested cross-validation would measure how stable the selected model is across samples. The pipeline itself carries over to the neural-network regression of the next project: scaling fitted inside each fold, a learning rate set from the largest Hessian eigenvalue, and a decaying SGD schedule. There, no closed-form solution exists to check the optimizers against.
