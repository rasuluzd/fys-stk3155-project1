\title{Regression, resampling and gradient descent for the Runge function}
\author{Rasul Ruslanovitsj {\O}zdber}
\affiliation{Department of Physics, University of Oslo, Norway}
\date{October 5, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Polynomial regression is the simplest setting in which model complexity, regularization and the reliability of error estimates can be studied exactly. We fitted Runge's function, the classic example of polynomial instability near the ends of an interval, from 100 noisy observations, and asked when regularization helps and how reliably resampling and gradient methods identify a suitable model. We implemented ordinary least squares, Ridge, the bootstrap and plain, momentum, adaptive and stochastic gradient descent, and used scikit-learn for cross-validation and reference fits and Autograd for automatic derivatives. Ten-fold cross-validation selected a degree-12 OLS fit with MSE $0.0173\pm0.0014$; on 2000 independent observations its MSE was 0.0113, close to the noise variance 0.01, and nearly unregularized Ridge performed identically. High-degree fits failed mainly where they extrapolated beyond the outermost training points, and these few points dominated single-split test errors and small-sample bootstrap variances; Ridge suppressed this instability. Momentum reached a fixed coefficient tolerance fastest, and Adam was the least sensitive to the learning rate. Complexity should therefore be chosen by cross-validated error, and optimizers compared at a stated accuracy and cost.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
\emph{The abstract and this section contain LLM-assisted text (level 3); see Appendix~\ref{app:llm}.}

Fitting a function to noisy observations is the basic task of supervised learning, and polynomial regression is its simplest instance. The model is linear in its parameters, so ordinary least squares (OLS) and Ridge regression have closed-form solutions against which every other method can be checked \cite{HjorthJensen2026,Hastie2009}. Runge's function $f(x)=1/(1+25x^2)$ on $[-1,1]$ is the classic test case: Runge showed that interpolating it with polynomials at equally spaced points diverges near the ends of the interval as the degree grows \cite{Runge1901}. With noisy, randomly placed data the same sensitivity appears as variance. A high-degree polynomial is pinned down only by the few observations near $\pm1$, and where it must extrapolate it can swing far from the data. Because $f$ is known, the bias and the variance of a fit can be measured directly rather than only estimated, which makes the problem a laboratory for the bias--variance trade-off that governs every later model in the course.

Three groups of methods address this trade-off. Ridge \cite{Hoerl1970} and Lasso \cite{Tibshirani1996} regression penalize large coefficients and exchange variance for bias. The bootstrap \cite{Efron1979} and $k$-fold cross-validation \cite{Hastie2009} estimate the test error without new data and are used to select the degree and the penalty. Gradient descent replaces the closed-form solutions, with momentum \cite{Polyak1964}, the adaptive rules AdaGrad \cite{Duchi2011}, RMSprop \cite{Tieleman2012} and Adam \cite{Kingma2015}, and stochastic mini-batches \cite{Robbins1951}. It is the optimizer of the neural networks in the next project, where no closed form exists.

Following Project~1 \cite{Project1_2026}, we asked four questions: how polynomial degree, sample size and noise set the trade-off for OLS; when Ridge and Lasso improve on OLS; whether the bootstrap and cross-validation select the same complexity; and how efficiently the gradient methods recover the closed-form solutions. We implemented OLS and Ridge through the singular value decomposition, the bootstrap, the gradient-based optimizers and the subgradient Lasso ourselves. Scikit-learn supplied cross-validation and reference fits \cite{Pedregosa2011}, and Autograd supplied automatic derivatives \cite{Maclaurin2015}.

Section~\ref{sec:theory} presents the models, the bias--variance decomposition and the optimizers. Section~\ref{sec:implementation} describes the implementation and its tests against closed-form and library results. Section~\ref{sec:results} answers parts a--i, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} summarizes the findings, their limitations and possible extensions. The code, settings and saved results are at \url{https://github.com/rasuluzd/fys-stk3155-project1}.

\subsection{Data, scaling and error measures}\label{sec:data}\label{sec:scaling}
Following part a, we generated
\begin{equation}
y_i=f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i,\quad f(x)=\frac{1}{1+25x^2},\quad
\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).
\end{equation}
Unless specified otherwise, 100 inputs were drawn uniformly from $[-1,1]$, with $\sigma=0.1$, seed 2026 and an 80/20 training/test split. We fitted polynomials through degree 15 and extended the sensitivity and model-selection studies to degree 20.

For degree $p$, the raw features are $A_{ij}=x_i^j$, $j=1,\ldots,p$. Using only the training observations, we computed column means $\mu_j$, standard deviations $s_j$ and target mean $\bar y$. The fitted prediction is
\begin{equation}
\widehat y(x)=\bar y+\sum_{j=1}^p\theta_j\frac{x^j-\mu_j}{s_j}.
\end{equation}
% Evidence: part_a.json/scaling (column standard deviations); benchmarks.json (scaled vs centred OLS).
Centering removes the intercept from the fit, so it is never penalized; the raw intercept is $\bar y-\sum_j\theta_j\mu_j/s_j$ \cite[Sec.~3.13]{HjorthJensen2026}. Standardization matters less here than for most data: on $[-1,1]$ every monomial is bounded by one, and at degree 15 the column standard deviations lie between 0.11 and 0.50. OLS predictions are in fact invariant to rescaling the columns; with and without standardization they agreed to $1.3\times10^{-13}$. Scaling matters for two other reasons. The Ridge and Lasso penalties act on coefficient sizes, which become comparable only after standardization, and the stable step size of gradient descent is set by the spectrum of $X^TX$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:gd_theory}). Means and scales are computed from the training observations only and then applied to the test data. They are refitted inside every bootstrap sample and CV fold, because statistics that include held-out points would leak information about them into the fit. Scaling cannot remove the strong correlations between neighbouring powers of $x$, so the design stays ill-conditioned at high degree.

On each evaluation set we used
\begin{equation}
\MSE=\frac1n\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2,\qquad
R^2=1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}.
\end{equation}
Here $\bar y$ is the mean of that evaluation set. Negative $R^2$ means predictions are worse than its constant mean \cite[Sec.~3.5]{HjorthJensen2026}.

\subsection{OLS, Ridge and Lasso}
Write $X$ for the standardized training features, $y$ for the centered target and $n$ for the training size. The three costs are
\begin{align}
C_{\rm OLS}&=\frac1n\|y-X\theta\|^2,\nonumber\\
C_{\rm Ridge}&=C_{\rm OLS}+\lambda\|\theta\|_2^2,\nonumber\\
C_{\rm Lasso}&=C_{\rm OLS}+\lambda\|\theta\|_1.
\end{align}
The OLS gradient $-\frac2nX^T(y-X\theta)$ vanishes when $X^TX\theta=X^Ty$. Ridge adds $2\lambda\theta$ to the gradient and hence $n\lambda I$ to $X^TX$ \cite{Hoerl1970}. The solutions are
\begin{align}
\widehat\theta_{\rm OLS}&=X^+y,\nonumber\\
\widehat\theta_{\rm Ridge}&=(X^TX+n\lambda I)^{-1}X^Ty.
\end{align}
We evaluated these using the pseudoinverse/SVD, without explicitly inverting $X^TX$. For $X=UDV^T$, Ridge multiplies each OLS singular-direction coefficient by
\begin{equation}
q_j=\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+n\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Small singular values are damped most strongly, reducing variance at the cost of bias \cite[Secs.~3.3 and 3.8]{HjorthJensen2026}. Lasso can set slopes exactly to zero, but has no general closed form \cite{Tibshirani1996}, \cite[Sec.~3.9]{HjorthJensen2026}. Our mean-loss convention requires scikit-learn parameters $\alpha=n\lambda$ for Ridge and $\alpha=\lambda/2$ for Lasso.

A Lasso solution is characterized by the stationarity conditions $g_j+\lambda\operatorname{sign}(\theta_j)=0$ for nonzero slopes and $|g_j|\leq\lambda$ for zero slopes, where $g$ is the gradient of the data term \cite[Sec.~3.9]{HjorthJensen2026}. We used scikit-learn's coordinate-descent fits as references only when these conditions and the duality gap were satisfied to $10^{-7}$ \cite{SklearnLasso}.

\subsection{Bias--variance decomposition and resampling}\label{sec:biasvar_theory}\label{sec:resampling}
Let $g_D(x)$ be the fit obtained from a random training set $D$, so that $\tilde y=g_D(x)$ in the notation of the project, and let $m(x)=\E_D[g_D(x)]$ be the mean fit. At a fixed input $x$ a new observation is $Y=f(x)+\varepsilon$, with $\E[\varepsilon]=0$, $\operatorname{var}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2$ and $\varepsilon$ independent of $D$. Adding and subtracting $m$ gives
\[
Y-g_D=(f-m)+(m-g_D)+\varepsilon,
\]
and squaring gives
\begin{align}
(Y-g_D)^2={}&(f-m)^2+(m-g_D)^2+\varepsilon^2\nonumber\\
&+2(f-m)(m-g_D)+2(f-m)\varepsilon\nonumber\\
&+2(m-g_D)\varepsilon .
\end{align}
We take the expectation over both $D$ and $\varepsilon$. Since $f-m$ does not depend on $D$, the first cross term averages to $2(f-m)\,\E_D[m-g_D]=0$ by the definition of $m$. The second vanishes because $\E[\varepsilon]=0$. The third vanishes because $\varepsilon$ is independent of $D$, so $\E[(m-g_D)\varepsilon]=\E_D[m-g_D]\,\E[\varepsilon]=0$. Hence
\begin{equation}
\E_{D,\varepsilon}[(Y-g_D)^2]
=\underbrace{(f-m)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
+\underbrace{\E_D[(g_D-m)^2]}_{\text{variance}}+\sigma^2.
\label{eq:biasvar}
\end{equation}
Averaging each term over the evaluation inputs gives the decomposition $\E[(y-\tilde y)^2]=\mathrm{Bias}^2[\tilde y]+\operatorname{var}[\tilde y]+\sigma^2$ of the project, Eq.~(2.52) of the lecture notes \cite[Sec.~2.10]{HjorthJensen2026}. The squared bias is the systematic error of the mean fit, the variance measures how much the fit changes from one training set to another, and $\sigma^2$ is the noise in the new observation, which no model can remove. The expectation $\E_D$ is over training sets, that is, over both the random input locations and the training noise.

The bootstrap estimates training-set variation by resampling paired training observations with replacement \cite{Efron1979}. A resample contains on average only $1-(1-1/n)^n\approx63\%$ of the distinct observations, so each observation is missing from about 37\% of the resamples. Each resample is fitted and predicted on the same fixed test inputs \cite[Sec.~2.11]{HjorthJensen2026}, following week~36, Exercises~2--3 \cite{Exercises36}. With $B$ predictions $g_{bi}$ and their mean $\bar g_i$, the stored arrays obey
\begin{align}
\frac1{n_t B}\sum_{i,b}(y_i-g_{bi})^2
={}&\frac1{n_t}\sum_i(y_i-\bar g_i)^2\nonumber\\
&+\frac1{n_t B}\sum_{i,b}(g_{bi}-\bar g_i)^2.
\end{align}
The first term uses noisy $y$, so it includes the noise contribution in expectation. Its difference from the same quantity using known $f$ is
\[
\frac1{n_t}\sum_i\left[\varepsilon_i^2+2\varepsilon_i(f_i-\bar g_i)\right].
\]
This averages to $\sigma^2$ over independent test noise, but need not equal it for one fixed test set. We show both versions.

In $k$-fold CV, each fold is predicted by a model fitted to the other $k-1$ folds \cite[Ch.~7]{Hastie2009}. We used shuffled five- and ten-fold splits, identical across candidates, and selected the lowest mean validation MSE \cite[Secs.~2.12 and 3.14]{HjorthJensen2026}. Fold standard errors are descriptive because training folds overlap.

\subsection{Gradient methods}\label{sec:gd_theory}
The analytical gradients and Ridge Hessian are
\begin{align}
\nabla C_{\rm OLS}&=\frac2nX^T(X\theta-y),\nonumber\\
\nabla C_{\rm Ridge}&=\nabla C_{\rm OLS}+2\lambda\theta,\nonumber\\
H&=\frac2nX^TX+2\lambda I.
\end{align}
Plain GD updates $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\nabla C(\theta_k)$. Each quadratic error component is multiplied by $1-\eta h_j$, so convergence requires $0<\eta<2/h_{\max}$ on a positive-definite problem. The optimal constant step size is $2/(h_{\max}+h_{\min})$ \cite[Secs.~4.3--4.5]{HjorthJensen2026}; week~37, Exercise~4 connects this bound to GD and AD \cite{Exercises37}.

Write $g_k=\nabla C(\theta_{k-1})$ and subtract the step $d_k$ from $\theta_{k-1}$. The implemented updates are
\begin{align}
\text{Momentum:}\quad d_k&=\beta d_{k-1}+\eta g_k,\nonumber\\
\text{AdaGrad:}\quad a_k&=a_{k-1}+g_k^2,\nonumber\\
\text{RMSprop:}\quad a_k&=\rho a_{k-1}+(1-\rho)g_k^2.
\end{align}
Both adaptive steps are $d_k=\eta g_k/(\sqrt{a_k}+\epsilon)$. AdaGrad's denominator keeps growing; RMSprop forgets old squared gradients. Adam instead uses
\begin{align}
m_k&=\beta_1m_{k-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_k,\nonumber\\
v_k&=\beta_2v_{k-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_k^2,\nonumber\\
d_k&=\eta\frac{m_k/(1-\beta_1^k)}{\sqrt{v_k/(1-\beta_2^k)}+\epsilon}.
\end{align}
All operations are componentwise, state starts at zero and $k=1$ on the first update. Momentum \cite{Polyak1964} retains the previous step; AdaGrad \cite{Duchi2011} and RMSprop \cite{Tieleman2012} rescale each coordinate by its gradient history; Adam \cite{Kingma2015} combines both ideas and corrects its initially small moving averages. We used $\beta=0.9$, $\rho=0.99$, $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.999)$ and $\epsilon=10^{-8}$, outside the square root, as in week~38's Tuesday implementation \cite[Secs.~4.6 and 4.8--4.11]{HjorthJensen2026}. The comparisons follow week~37, Exercise~6 and week~38, Exercises~1--3 \cite{Exercises37,Exercises38,Week38Tuesday}.

Autograd differentiates the implemented scalar cost by applying the chain rule to the elementary operations recorded while the cost is evaluated \cite{Maclaurin2015,Baydin2018}. This is neither symbolic differentiation, which manipulates expressions and can produce very large formulas, nor numerical differentiation, which perturbs the inputs and suffers from truncation and round-off error: AD gives the exact derivative of the program, up to floating-point rounding. In reverse mode one forward and one backward sweep give all $p$ partial derivatives at a cost of a small constant multiple of one cost evaluation, independent of $p$ \cite{Baydin2018}, \cite[Sec.~4.14]{HjorthJensen2026}. We used Autograd rather than JAX because it differentiates the NumPy cost functions directly and works in 64-bit precision by default, so the analytical and automatic gradients can be compared at machine precision without extra configuration.

For Lasso we add $\lambda\operatorname{sign}(\theta)$ to the OLS gradient and choose $\operatorname{sign}(0)=0$. This is a valid subgradient because the subdifferential of $|\theta|$ at zero is $[-1,1]$; the value 1 that JAX returns in the project notebook is an equally valid choice \cite{Project1_2026}. A fixed-step subgradient method does not, however, produce exact zeros at a finite iteration.

SGD \cite{Robbins1951} reshuffles the training observations each epoch and applies the same update rules to mini-batch gradients, dividing the data term by the actual batch size. Optimizer state persists between batches. We compared constant rates with $\eta_t=\eta_0t_1/(t+t_1)$, where $t$ counts updates from zero \cite[Sec.~4.7]{HjorthJensen2026}, as in week~38, Exercise~4 and week~39's SGD examples \cite{Exercises38,HjorthJensen2026week39}.

\section{Implementation and verification}\label{sec:implementation}
\emph{Contains LLM-assisted paragraphs (level 3); see Appendix~\ref{app:llm}.}

The repository separates \texttt{regression.py}, \texttt{resampling.py} and \texttt{optimizers.py}; task scripts write JSON results and PDF figures. The numerical work uses NumPy, scikit-learn and Matplotlib \cite{Harris2020,Pedregosa2011,Hunter2007}. The README lists dependencies, all scripts and reproduction commands. Numerical claims below identify their saved results in source comments.

Every fit in the resampling experiments follows the same four steps, and the order of the steps is what keeps held-out data out of the fit:
\begin{enumerate}
\item choose the training rows: an 80/20 split, a bootstrap resample or $k-1$ CV folds;
\item build the columns $x,\dots,x^p$ and compute their means and standard deviations, and the mean of $y$, from these rows only;
\item fit $\theta$ on the scaled training rows, by the SVD, a gradient method or scikit-learn;
\item transform the held-out rows with the training statistics, predict and store the error.
\end{enumerate}
All gradient methods share one driver loop in \texttt{optimizers.py}; only the update rule changes:
{\small
\begin{verbatim}
for k in range(n_iter):
    g = gradient(theta)        # analytic or AD
    theta = theta - opt.update(g, eta)
    if rel_error(theta, theta_ref) < tol:
        break
\end{verbatim}}
\noindent Here \texttt{opt.update} returns $\eta g$ for plain GD and the steps $d_k$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:gd_theory} for the other rules, and SGD runs the same update on each mini-batch.

% Evidence: benchmarks.json; part_d.json/own_vs_sklearn.
The 27 tests compare custom routines with closed-form or library answers. OLS predictions agreed with scikit-learn to $7.6\times10^{-14}$, and Ridge coefficients agreed to reported precision. Analytical and AD gradients agreed to a relative $1.6\times10^{-15}$. Full-batch SGD reproduced GD within $2.2\times10^{-16}$, and our $k$-fold code reproduced scikit-learn's fold scores to a relative $1.2\times10^{-9}$ when folds and preprocessing matched. The OLS library reference uses the same relative singular-value cutoff, $10^{-15}$, as the custom pseudoinverse.

The GD/SGD comparisons start at zero; the library Lasso penalty paths use warm starts. Unless stated otherwise, convergence means relative coefficient error $r=\|\theta-\widehat\theta\|/\|\widehat\theta\|<10^{-6}$. This measures agreement with a known solution; it is distinct from test error. The README lists the software versions used.

\subsection{a) Ordinary least squares}\label{sec:res_ols}

\begin{figure}[tb]

\begin{figure*}[tb]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{a_coefficients.pdf}
\caption{The standardized OLS coefficient norm grows from 2.70 at degree 6 to 856 at degree 15. Large, cancelling slopes indicate sensitivity to small changes in the observations.}\label{fig:coef}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:mse_r2} shows the pattern expected from the bias--variance trade-off \cite[Sec.~2.10]{HjorthJensen2026}. The training error falls with every added degree, because each model contains the previous one, while the test error first drops and then becomes erratic at high degree. On the main split, degrees 6 and 12 give practically the same test MSE (0.0190 and 0.0189); with only 20 test points we cannot say that one is better.

Figure~\ref{fig:edge} shows where the high-degree fits go wrong. Three of the 20 test points lie to the left of the smallest training input, $x=-0.953$, so every model has to extrapolate there, and that is exactly where a high-degree polynomial is least constrained. These three points account for 89\% and 92\% of the test error at degrees 13 and 15. Without them, the test MSE stays between 0.014 and 0.018 for every degree from 8 to 15. The spikes are therefore the Runge phenomenon at the edge of the interval \cite{Runge1901}, not a general loss of accuracy.

The coefficients tell the same story (Fig.~\ref{fig:coef}). The norm of the standardized coefficients grows from 2.7 at degree 6 to 856 at degree 15: the fit becomes a balance between large terms of opposite sign, and small changes in the data shift that balance. Standardization does not prevent this. It lowers the condition number of the degree-15 design only from $4.7\times10^5$ to $1.9\times10^5$, because the powers $x^j$ remain strongly correlated on $[-1,1]$.

Repeating the experiment on 200 data sets per setting shows how the best degree depends on the data. With $\sigma=0.1$, the degree with the lowest median test MSE rose from 10 for 50 points to 18 for 1000 points; with 100 points, it fell from 16 to 8 when the noise increased from 0.05 to 0.3. More data support a more complex model, and more noise favours a simpler one. We report medians because a few unstable fits make the mean very large.

\subsection{b) Ridge regression}\label{sec:res_ridge}
% Evidence: part_b.json/main, svd_degree15; ridge_supplement.json/main, ensemble.
\begin{figure*}[tb]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{b_ridge.pdf}
\caption{Fixed Ridge penalties keep the high-degree test error bounded and shrink the coefficients by orders of magnitude, at the price of a higher training error for large $\lambda$. (a) Test MSE, (b) training MSE and (c) norm of the standardized coefficients against degree for OLS and four penalties, all on the seed-2026 split.}\label{fig:ridge}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:ridge} repeats the analysis of part a for four fixed penalties. Ridge keeps the high-degree test error bounded: at degree 20, OLS reaches a test MSE of about $9\times10^3$, while Ridge with $\lambda=10^{-3}$ stays at 0.037. At degree 15 the same penalty shrinks the coefficient norm from 856 to 1.8. The price is visible in panel (b): the training error rises with $\lambda$, as it must, since OLS minimizes exactly that error \cite[Eq.~(3.91)]{HjorthJensen2026}. Test $R^2$ is not shown, because on a fixed test set it equals $1-\MSE/\operatorname{var}(y)$.

The singular values explain why the penalty works. At degree 15 they range from 27.6 down to $1.5\times10^{-4}$. Ridge multiplies the component along each singular direction by $d_j^2/(d_j^2+n\lambda)$ \cite[Eqs.~(3.48) and (3.95)]{HjorthJensen2026}, so the directions with tiny singular values, which carry the unstable wiggles of a high-degree fit, are suppressed first. On the main split, a grid of 45 penalties gave its lowest test MSE, 0.0135, at degree 10 and $\lambda\approx2\times10^{-6}$, compared with 0.0189 for the best OLS fit. We treat this as exploratory; the final choice in part i is made by cross-validation.

Whether shrinkage helps depends on how well the data already constrain the fit. In 40 paired data sets per setting, degree-20 Ridge with $\lambda=10^{-7}$ lowered the median test MSE from 0.236 to 0.018 with 50 points and was better in 38 of the 40 runs. With 1000 points, OLS and Ridge were almost equal (0.0105 and 0.0108), and Ridge was better in only 3 of 40 runs. Without noise, Ridge was clearly worse (0.0010 against $6\times10^{-5}$). Shrinkage pays off when the variance dominates and only adds bias when it does not. Ridge hardly moves the optimum itself: with 100 points, the median test MSE is lowest at degree 12 for OLS and at degree 13 for $\lambda=10^{-7}$, while $\lambda=10^{-3}$ makes the curve almost flat, between 0.020 and 0.022 for all degrees from 8 to 20. What Ridge buys is a wide and safe range of degrees, not a different best degree.

\subsection{c) Bias, variance and bootstrap}\label{sec:res_biasvar}

Figure~\ref{fig:hastie} is our version of Fig.~2.11 in Ref.~\cite{Hastie2009}: 100 independent training sets of 80 points, each scored on the same 2000 test points. The mean training error falls steadily, while the mean test error has its minimum at degree 8 (0.0166) and then rises. The median test error is lowest at degree 12 (0.0134); the mean is pulled up by the occasional training set that produces a very poor high-degree fit.

With 800 training points, Fig.~\ref{fig:biasvar}(b) shows the textbook picture of Eq.~(\ref{eq:biasvar}). The squared bias, measured against the known $f$, falls with degree, the variance grows slowly, and the bootstrap variance agrees with the variance over 400 fresh training sets ($1.79\times10^{-4}$ and $1.81\times10^{-4}$ at degree 12). Measuring the bias against the noisy $y$ instead of $f$ adds the noise: the difference is 0.0103, close to $\sigma^2=0.01$, as the derivation in Sec.~\ref{sec:biasvar_theory} predicts. The fresh training sets also show where the optimum lies. With 80 training points their error is lowest at degree 8, exactly where the squared bias (0.0047) and the variance (0.0060) cross, as Eq.~(\ref{eq:biasvar}) suggests; with 800 points the error is almost flat from degree 16 to 20 and lowest at the end of the grid.

With only 80 training points (panel a), the bootstrap is far from the fresh-set reference. It selects degree 4 instead of 8, and at degree 8 its variance is 7.75 rather than 0.006. The cause is again the three extrapolated test points of part a: from degree 7 upward they produce more than 99.9\% of the bootstrap variance. Without them, the degree-8 variance is 0.0044 and the bootstrap error is lowest at degree 8, in agreement with the reference. The mechanism lies in the resampling itself. Each training point is missing from a resample with probability $(1-1/80)^{80}\approx0.37$, and a resample that loses the leftmost points must extrapolate further, which a high-degree polynomial amplifies. The bootstrap can only explore the one training set we have, and that set covers the left edge poorly; fresh training sets redraw the inputs.

\subsection{d) Cross-validation}\label{sec:res_cv}
% Evidence: part_d.json/ols,ridge,own_vs_sklearn,reshuffle,leakage; ridge_supplement.json/ensemble.
\begin{figure*}[tb]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{d_cross_validation.pdf}
\caption{Both CV choices select degree 12, while bootstrap selects degree 4 on the original small sample. Left: OLS errors and descriptive fold-standard-error bands. Right: ten-fold Ridge errors. Scaling is fitted inside each fold. The vertical limit omits degree-20 CV MSEs of 1782 and 7.98.}\label{fig:cv}
\end{figure*}

Five- and ten-fold cross-validation both select degree 12 for OLS, with CV MSE 0.020 and 0.017 (Fig.~\ref{fig:cv}). For Ridge, ten-fold CV picks degree 12 with the smallest penalty in the grid, $\lambda=10^{-10}$, which is effectively OLS, and five-fold CV picks degree 11 with $\lambda=10^{-6}$. On this data set, regularization is not needed once the degree is chosen well.

CV and the small-sample bootstrap disagree (degree 12 against 4), but they do not measure the same thing. The bootstrap evaluates refits on the 20 fixed test points, including the three extrapolated ones, and each refit sees only about 63\% of the distinct training points. CV trains on 80--90\% of all 100 observations and evaluates every observation once. Removing the three extrapolated points moves the bootstrap choice to degree 8 (part c), so the disagreement comes from extrapolation and not from a different balance between bias and variance in the interior. Because the scaling is fitted inside each fold, nothing from the held-out fold reaches the fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling}). We also measured what leakage would cost here: standardizing all 100 points before splitting left the degree-12 OLS CV error unchanged, as the invariance of OLS predictions to rescaling requires, and changed the Ridge CV error by less than 1\%. On $[-1,1]$, where all columns already have similar sizes, the effect is small, but the order of the steps is what makes the estimate honest.

The selected degree is itself uncertain. Over 20 different random fold assignments it ranged from 4 to 13 with five folds and from 10 to 13 with ten folds, with median 12, and the one-standard-error rule \cite[Sec.~3.14]{HjorthJensen2026} also gives degree 12. The robust result is therefore the error level near the minimum, about 0.017, rather than one particular degree.

\subsection{e) Gradient descent and automatic derivatives}\label{sec:res_gd}
% Evidence: part_e.json/gradient_check,timing,degree6; part_a.json/scaling (Hessian spectra with and without standardization).
The analytical and automatic gradients agree to a relative $1.2\times10^{-15}$ for OLS and Ridge at degrees 6 and 15, that is, to machine precision. Inside gradient descent they give identical runs: 14\,399 iterations for degree-6 OLS, with final coefficients that differ by $4\times10^{-15}$. One AD gradient cost 16 cost evaluations at $n=80$ but only 2.8 at $n=10^6$. The large-$n$ value is the small constant factor expected for reverse mode; at small $n$ the bookkeeping of the AD library dominates.

The learning rate is governed by the Hessian $H=\frac2nX^TX$. For degree-6 OLS its eigenvalues range from 7.70 down to 0.0037, so gradient descent is stable only for $\eta<2/h_{\max}=0.260$ \cite[Eq.~(4.20)]{HjorthJensen2026}. At the optimal rate $2/(h_{\max}+h_{\min})$ the error shrinks by the factor $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ per step, with $\kappa=2090$ \cite[Eq.~(4.26)]{HjorthJensen2026}. This predicts about $(\kappa/2)\ln 10^6\approx14\,400$ iterations to reach $r<10^{-6}$, and we needed 14\,399. Rates above the bound diverged along the steepest direction, and smaller rates were slower. Standardization matters for this count: with centering only, the degree-6 Hessian has $\kappa\approx4600$, more than twice the standardized value, but at degree 15 $\kappa$ is of order $10^{10}$ either way ($9\times10^{10}$ and $3\times10^{10}$), because the columns stay nearly collinear. Ridge with $\lambda=0.1$ adds $2\lambda$ to every eigenvalue, lowers $\kappa$ to 39 and converges in 244 iterations: regularization helps the optimizer as well as the statistics.

Every method got the same data, the same starting point and the same grid of 28 learning rates, with a budget of $10^5$ iterations (Table~\ref{tab:opt}, Fig.~\ref{fig:optimizers}). Momentum was by far the fastest: 656 iterations for OLS, against 17\,361 for plain GD on the same grid and 14\,399 for GD at its optimal rate. The lecture notes explain the gain: with optimally tuned parameters, momentum needs a number of iterations proportional to $\sqrt\kappa$ instead of $\kappa$ \cite[Eq.~(4.33)]{HjorthJensen2026}, and $\sqrt{2090}\approx46$. Our fixed $\beta=0.9$ is not optimal, so the gain we observe, a factor of 22, is smaller. Momentum's best rate, 0.46, also lies above the plain-GD bound of 0.26, which does not apply to the momentum iteration. With Ridge ($\lambda=10^{-3}$) every method needed fewer iterations, as expected from the smaller condition number.

Adam converged at all 28 rates, which makes it the safest choice when a good rate is unknown, but it needed 1144 iterations. AdaGrad slowed down as its sum of squared gradients grew. RMSprop never reached the target with a constant rate: its error stalled at a level proportional to the rate, from $4.5\times10^{-4}$ at $\eta=10^{-3}$ to $1.4\times10^{-2}$ at $\eta=0.03$. Near the minimum the scaled step $g_k/\sqrt{a_k}$ does not shrink, so the iterate keeps jumping by about $\eta$; a decaying rate reduced the error to $1.5\times10^{-5}$, still above the target.

The adaptive methods do not beat momentum here because they rescale each coordinate separately. They act as a diagonal preconditioner \cite[Eq.~(4.57)]{HjorthJensen2026}, which helps when the Hessian is close to diagonal. For polynomial features it is strongly non-diagonal, since neighbouring powers of $x$ are almost parallel; the lecture notes show the same failure on a rotated quadratic bowl. At degree 10, where $\kappa$ is far larger, none of the five methods reached the target within 50\,000 iterations.

\subsection{g) Lasso with gradient updates}\label{sec:res_lasso}

Momentum & 0.0316 & $1.61\times10^{-7}$ & 0.00353 \\

\end{table}

Autograd returns 0 as the derivative of $|\theta|$ at $\theta=0$, a valid subgradient, and our analytical gradient uses the same convention. We ran all five update rules for 40\,000 iterations on the degree-10 problem with $\lambda=10^{-3}$, chose each method's rate from a fixed grid of eight values using the training objective only, and compared with scikit-learn's coordinate-descent solution (Table~\ref{tab:lasso}). Adam came closest, with a relative coefficient error of $3\times10^{-5}$, followed by momentum ($3.5\times10^{-3}$); plain GD and AdaGrad remained 16--19\% away.

None of the gradient methods produced exact zeros, while the reference set three coefficients exactly to zero. This is expected. A subgradient step moves a coefficient past zero rather than onto it, whereas coordinate descent applies the soft-thresholding rule \cite[Eq.~(3.63)]{HjorthJensen2026}, which returns exactly zero whenever the data term is weak enough. Runge's function is even, but our random sample is not symmetric, so the odd coefficients need not vanish.

Test error is a poor measure of convergence. After 10\,000 GD steps at degree 10, OLS had a relative coefficient error of 99\% and yet a lower test MSE (0.0199) than the exact OLS solution (0.0370): stopping early acted like regularization on this split. The Adam fits for Ridge and Lasso matched their reference test MSEs to four digits. Whether an optimizer has converged must therefore be checked on the coefficients or the objective, not on the test error.

\subsection{h) Stochastic gradient descent}\label{sec:res_sgd}
% Evidence: part_h.json/setup,batch_size,methods_M10.
\begin{figure*}[tb]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{h_sgd.pdf}
\caption{Decaying rates reduce stochastic fluctuations, but can slow late progress. Degree-6 OLS uses 80 training observations, five shuffle seeds and separately tuned rates. Left: batch-size comparison. Right: five updates at batch size 10. Curves are smoothed in logarithmic epoch bins; the horizontal line is a reference scale, not a stopping guarantee.}\label{fig:sgd}
\end{figure*}

We ran SGD on degree-6 OLS for 2000 epochs with batch sizes 1, 5, 20 and 80, tuned each rate on the training cost and averaged over five shufflings (Fig.~\ref{fig:sgd}). With a constant rate, SGD does not settle at the minimum but fluctuates around it, because each mini-batch gradient is a noisy estimate of the full gradient. The size of this fluctuation is proportional to $\eta/M$ for batch size $M$ \cite[Eq.~(4.50)]{HjorthJensen2026}, so it can only be removed by a decreasing rate or larger batches. The decaying schedule $\eta_t=\eta_0t_1/(t+t_1)$ reduced the final excess cost for batch size 5 almost tenfold, from $4.4\times10^{-4}$ to $4.7\times10^{-5}$. The batch size also changes the stable rate. Applying Eq.~(4.20) of the lecture notes to a single observation, whose Hessian $2x_ix_i^T$ has largest eigenvalue $2\|x_i\|^2$, up to 127 here, gives $\eta<0.016$ for batch size 1, against 0.26 for the full batch; the tuned rates, 0.004 and 0.25, respect both limits.

Mini-batches did not save time here. One epoch of SGD costs the same number of operations as one full gradient step \cite[Eq.~(4.38)]{HjorthJensen2026}, but with batch size 1 it consists of 80 small updates, each with its own Python overhead: 2000 epochs took 0.87~s with batch size 1 and 0.045~s with the full batch. SGD pays off for large data sets, where a single full gradient is expensive; with 80 observations it does not.

Among the update rules at batch size 10, Adam reached the lowest coefficient error, 0.020 after 2000 epochs, but took about twice as long as plain SGD (0.23 against 0.12~s). For a problem of this size the SVD solution is both exact and cheapest; among the iterative methods, momentum is the better choice when high accuracy is required.

For the final comparison we used ten-fold CV over degrees 1--20, with 21 Ridge penalties ($10^{-10}$ to 1) and seven Lasso penalties ($10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$). The selected models were refitted on all 100 points and only then evaluated on 2000 new observations (seed 2027), so the test set played no role in the selection.

At $\sigma=0.1$, degree-12 OLS and Ridge with a negligible penalty give the same CV score, $0.0173\pm0.0014$, and the same test MSE, 0.0113 (Table~\ref{tab:final}). We therefore prefer OLS, which has one tuning parameter fewer. Lasso is slightly worse, and its selected penalty lies at the edge of the grid, so a smaller penalty might still improve it. At $\sigma=0.3$ the selected degrees drop to 8--10 and Ridge has the lowest CV score, but the three methods differ by much less than the fold uncertainty.

The CV error, 0.0173, is clearly above the test error, 0.0113. This is mostly the noise in our particular sample: the 100 observations have a realized noise variance of 0.0139, against 0.0101 for the 2000 new points. Relative to the true function the errors are small in both cases, 0.0023 for the fold fits and 0.0016 for the refit. The selected model is thus within about 0.002 of $f$ in mean square, and the rest of the error is noise that no model can remove.

Which term of Eq.~(\ref{eq:biasvar}) does each method attack? We answered this with 150 independent training sets of 80 points at degree 15. For OLS, the squared bias was 0.0065 and the variance 0.21. Ridge and Lasso with $\lambda=3.16\times10^{-4}$ reduced the variance about sixtyfold, to 0.0035, while the squared bias stayed close to the OLS value (0.0067 and 0.0049). The penalties act on the variance; choosing a lower degree is the other way to control it, and nothing removes the noise term $\sigma^2$. That Lasso's measured bias is even below that of OLS is not significant: with a variance of 0.21, averaging only 150 fits inflates the OLS bias estimate by about $0.21/150\approx0.0014$.

All 3080 Lasso fits behind Table~\ref{tab:final} passed the convergence checks of Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}. With 100 observations, fold-to-fold variation and finite grids, the table does not support a strong general ranking of the three methods; on this problem their differences are smaller than the uncertainty.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
\emph{LLM-assisted text (level 3); see Appendix~\ref{app:llm}.}

Ten-fold cross-validation selected a degree-12 OLS fit whose MSE on 2000 independent observations was 0.0113, close to the noise variance; relative to the true function its mean-square error was only 0.0016, and nearly unregularized Ridge gave the same result. High-degree fits were accurate where the data constrained them and failed where they had to extrapolate, as Runge's analysis of polynomial interpolation leads one to expect. A few test inputs beyond the outermost training point produced the spikes in the single-split test error and essentially all of the small-sample bootstrap variance. Ridge removes this instability by damping the directions with small singular values. At degree 15 it reduced the variance by a factor of about 60 at almost no cost in bias, and at degree 6 it lowered the condition number seen by gradient descent from 2090 to 39. Its price, a bias towards smaller coefficients, mattered only when the data already constrained the fit. Lasso adds sparsity, but it has no closed form, its gradient solution needs soft thresholding to give exact zeros, and on this problem it did not clearly improve on OLS or Ridge. Among the optimizers, momentum reached the closed-form solution fastest and Adam was the least sensitive to the learning rate. Constant-rate RMSprop stalled at an error floor proportional to the rate, and mini-batches did not pay off for 80 observations.

These results have clear limitations. They rest on one random design of 100 points, and a single 20-point test split is unreliable: three extrapolated inputs dominated its ranking of the degrees. The bootstrap is conditional on one training set and inherited its poor coverage of the left edge, and the degree selected by five-fold CV varied from 4 to 13 between fold assignments. The optimizer rankings hold only for the stated rate grids, tolerance and budgets, and the Lasso penalty grid ended at the selected value. Our bias and variance estimates use the known $f$; on real data only the noisy-target version, which absorbs $\sigma^2$, can be computed.

Two extensions follow directly. Repeating the analysis with Chebyshev-distributed inputs, which cluster near $\pm1$, or with test inputs restricted to the training range would separate interpolation from extrapolation and test the edge interpretation quantitatively, and nested cross-validation would measure how stable the selected model is across samples. The pipeline itself carries over to the neural-network regression of the next project: scaling fitted inside each fold, a learning rate set from the largest Hessian eigenvalue, and a decaying SGD schedule. There, no closed-form solution exists to check the optimizers against.
